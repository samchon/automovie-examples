# Four medieval sword shorts

## Commission {#running-time}

User direction. Deliver one common brief and four individual, engrossing English screenplays. Give each its own one-minute beginning, encounter, reversal and ending. Research the medieval world and make its people move with weight, fatigue and purpose. The user requested a duel between knights, a moving mountain-road encounter between a knight and several bandits, an intense mounted fight with lances followed by maces, and one different fight. The writer owns the final dialogue, staging, beat lengths and titles within the following production direction.

## Time and place {#period}

The supervisor chose the Savoyard approaches to the Great St Bernard Pass around 1460. The hospice connects the settlements and the people who carry food between them. The anthology ranges from the Aosta valley floor to the high road and its northern approach. Each episode stands alone. In each film, keeping or breaking a promise costs someone something. The writer chooses who else is present when the promise is made. Give those costs different weights, from a saddle to the chance to carry their uncle for the last time.

The hospice accounts preserve actual deliveries of rye bread from Orsières up to the hospice in 1419, entries 1304–1306. They establish this traffic on the northern approach. An Italian visitor's polished, open-faced Milanese helmet can take its silhouette from a surviving example dated about 1460. These sources inform the production's invented people and events. [Hospice accounts, printed p. 73](https://doc.rero.ch/record/21653/files/I-N-268_1973_04_00.pdf), [Metropolitan Museum of Art, 42.50.15](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/27960), [congregation's archive and jurisdiction](https://gsbernard.ch/60/630.php).

## The saddle wager {#duel}

User direction supplies the one-on-one knightly encounter. The supervisor chose a friendly but humiliating wager in a Savoyard household yard. A visiting Italian knight once laughed at the split seat of the younger knight's saddle. Now the saddles are at stake and the first man outside their agreed line loses. They fight in earnest with blunted swords under their private bargain. The visitor steps out first, admits it before anyone else can, and must actually carry away the split saddle. The victory should leave the younger man laughing and the older man doing something more generous than the crowd expects.

## The bread road {#mountain-road}

User direction supplies a knight fighting several bandits while moving along a mountain road. The supervisor chose the ascent on the Orsières side and three robbers who want the bread their watchers saw being loaded. A knight has promised to see the carrier and the hospice's rye loaves up the road. The fight travels between recognizable bends. The robbers get away with the main load. The knight helps the carrier recover the few loaves left in the scree and resumes the climb beside the carrier under the diminished load. Give losing the bread a consequence that the audience can see.

Write an original mountain-road story from the bread accounts and this commission. The user wants the weight of the equipment, the loose ground and the cost of running to matter. A blow lands on Perrin's armour and he remains on his feet. Let a missed movement pass without the impact that contact would have caused. A startled response has its own cause. Give people time to check their pace, find their footing, breathe and move again. Footfalls, clothing and the load continue through those changes.

## The stolen mare {#mounted}

The user specified an intense mounted fight. Both riders use lances first. In this particular encounter a lance misses or glances off armour. Both then use maces. The lance encounter and the mace encounter each occupy a substantial stretch of screen time while both men remain in their saddles. Account for the lances, both maces and the horses' movement through the change.

The supervisor chose two armed household riders on the lower valley road. At muster, one has taken a mare promised to stay with her foal. Her keeper follows on his own mount. Their arms belong to their household service. The mare hears her foal from the paddock during or after the mounted mace encounter and turns back to her gate. The thief escapes on foot because the keeper chooses to settle the distressed reunited animals. Place both adult mounts at the ending. Let the horses and the watching keeper tell us why recovering this particular mare matters.

## The empty places {#fourth}

The fourth encounter was delegated. The supervisor chose armed hired men from two branches of one family fighting across a funeral procession in a valley lane. It gives the moving witnesses a power the crowd, robbers and riders in the other films do not possess. Two nephews promised the widow they would carry their uncle. Each gives up his place on the bier to draw a sword when the other side presses an old grievance. The widow and a mourner take the vacant places and the procession carries on. By the time the fighting ebbs, the two men have irretrievably missed the gate with the dead man. Make the old grievance legible in a line or an object. Let the audience hear the funeral recede while the men are still fighting.

## Screen action {#screen-action}

The supervisor chose a broad audience and four different registers: dry comedy, material loss, suspense resolving in animal tenderness, and grief. Keep what the audience can follow from the ground, faces, saddles, changes in speed, contact sounds and who still holds the burden after each encounter. Write cinematic action and its human consequences. Keep reenactment instructions, tactical methods and real-world attack details outside this commission. Dialogue and physical business must fit the allotted minute. The writer chooses the viewpoint, shots and whether people gather to watch each encounter.

## Cast appearances and references {#cast-appearance}

The user requests individual character descriptions and actual reference images. The common brief is the single collection in `docs/body/brief/`. Its cast domain holds one record per identifiable person or horse. The screenplay introduces the few visible details that let each one play this story. The character records give the fictional age, build, hair or coat, wardrobe or tack, and details that persist or change during the action. Anonymous members of the household and procession remain an ensemble. The uncle appears under linen on the bier. His covered silhouette and the worn leather toe visible at the hem are his casting references.

The supervisor chose individual wardrobe colours and silhouettes for the four households. The historical study identifies the period material used for comparison. Each cast record embeds its two casting photographs and records the visual check and selection for each view. The commissioning viewer selected all 48 photographs on the user’s behalf.

## First reading by the commissioning viewer {#commissioning-viewer}

The commissioning viewer recorded these responses to the four starting scripts for the director and writer.

The Better Seat. I felt the stable lad's thumb push through that torn saddle, and loved Carlo sending his fingers through the same hole at the end. At “First man over the line gets that one,” I want to see which seat he means. At “Mine. I was out,” I briefly heard him claiming victory. The saddle, eyes and boot outside the line could make the cost plain before anyone explains it.

Bread Cloths. Perrin letting the sack go to take Alix's weight is the moment that got me, and so did the last half-loaf. I want to be able to follow all three spilled loaves. Which one is still in whose hands? On this mountain road without a cart, how does the weight of the whole sack come into the bareheaded man's hands and travel away?

Answer Me. One broken cry from the foal makes a bigger choice than either rider. I wondered how two horses have time after their run to slow, curve, come to a stand, let two men dismount, and reunite in the 40–52 second span. When Rémy walks away with his coin, I want to understand what he has gained and lost, and what makes Luc open the hand around his sleeve.

The Last Carry. “Wait till I’ve got him,” and that last sight of the shoe beyond the gate were devastating. I want to check when each rear pole passes from one shoulder to the next, why the widow can no longer pull the cloth back down and whether the final splinter is close enough to see.
