# docs/research/kickboxing/styles.md: the two fighters' styles, their level and the learned tell

This study records how the two men of round one fight at championship level under GLORY rules: the pressure fighter B, the patient kicker A, the markers that separate their level from a club fighter's, how B's tell forms and how A reads and exploits it, the Brazilian kick that finishes the round, what landed kicks visibly cost during the round, and the conduct of championship fighters in the ring. It serves the brief: its cast records of A and B (style, repertoire, level, habits and tells, knowledge) and its record of the bout's level. Each technique's law, the exchange outcome table and activity rates belong to the screenplay's later study (see [scope](#scope)). The rule text the bout follows is in the [rules study](rules.md).

## How to read this study {#reading}

Each observation carries its kind in brackets:

- **[observation]**: what the original states, read directly in the saved copy. For a fight report this is the reporter's account of what he saw at ringside. For a coaching text it is the coach's account of practice. For a rulebook it is the rule's text.
- **[measurement]**: a value with its unit, method and group that the original reports.
- **[inference]**: this study's reasoning from the cited facts.
- **[choice]**: a decision this production may make where the sources leave room, offered to the brief.

Sources are cited by key with a locator. Web articles are located by their section heading, or by the opening words of the paragraph where the article has no headings. Papers are located by page, table or section. GLORY rule numbers carry the page number printed on the page. The research index lists every original, with its creator, URL, retrieval date, saved path, rights and the locators used, in its [styles section](../index.md#originals-styles).

Most coaching texts here are written for Muay Thai or MMA. GLORY kickboxing uses the same punches, kicks, knees, checks and blocks, but it forbids elbows, throws and sweeps, allows only one knee from a brief clinch, and scores no differently for kicks and punches. Each value or practice taken from Muay Thai, MMA, amateur WAKO or amateur K-1 states its translation where it is used.

## The pressure fighter {#pressure-fighter}

B is the defending champion and the pressure fighter. The "Dutch style" he fights in is the K-1 and GLORY heir of a Dutch school.

### Where the style comes from {#dutch-origin}

- Dutch kickboxing is "a specific, aggressive style developed in the 1970s and '80s by Dutch pioneers like Jan Plas, Rob Kaman, and later Ernesto Hoost and Ramon Dekkers", blending Kyokushin karate, boxing and Muay Thai into "high-volume punch-kick combinations, tight guards, and relentless pressure" (EV-DUTCH, para. beginning "Let's clear this up first") [observation].
- The lineage runs through the Mejiro (Jan Plas), Chakuriki (Thom Harinck) and Carbin gyms (FTX-DUTCH, section on the two systems, para. beginning "Dutch kickboxing blends") [observation]. The style "began to establish itself through K-1 in the 1990s" (ONE, para. beginning "Although it was a blend") [observation]. Dutch fighters "in recent history train more for K1 and Glory kickboxing rules" (BE, para. beginning "The Dutch version of Muay Thai") [observation].
- Its signature, as ONE describes two Dutch-Moroccan heavyweights: "strong punching, low kicking, and relentless pressure" (ONE, para. beginning "Their focus on strong punching") [observation].

### What he does {#pressure-repertoire}

- He chains boxing combinations into kicks. Low kicks come "in sequences, not as standalone strikes, but as finishers to combos", in flurries of four to six strikes such as "jab–cross–hook–low kick–body shot–head kick" (EV-DUTCH, section on modern fighters, items "Leg Kick Emphasis" and "High-Volume Combinations") [observation]. Classic patterns are "1–2–low kick, cross–hook–cross–body kick, and hook–low kick" (FTX-DUTCH, para. beginning "Muay Thai's money shots") [observation].
- GLORY's own coaching names the same finish: "Outside low kicks are usually thrown after a series of punches to finish off the combination" (GLK, para. beginning "Outside low kicks are usually thrown") [observation].
- The punches load the kick. Rob Kaman's cross, left hook, right low kick works because "in order to block the left hook, opponents have to put weight on the left foot and this makes it difficult to shin-block the incoming kick" (BE, "Rob Kaman", Technique #1) [observation]. Hoost's right hand loads the hips for the left hook or middle kick, and the left hook loads the right hand or the right low kick (HOOST, para. beginning "Explanation") [observation].
- He attacks the body with his hands. Dutch fighters combine low kicks with "body shots", and "Punches to the liver are often the target of choice" (BE, introduction, para. beginning "The Dutch version"; section "Fighting modes", item 1) [observation]. Dekkers' left hook, body hook, low kick lands the kick "from an angle that makes it very difficult for his opponent to notice or defend" (BE, "Ramon Dekkers", Technique #1) [observation].
- He counters kicks with kicks. "Counterattacking kicks with leg kicks is a common tactic in Dutch Muay Thai", catching the opponent "out of balance or in a compromised angle for a split second" (BE, "Ramon Dekkers", Technique #3) [observation]. Translation to GLORY: Dekkers grabs the kick before kicking the landing leg. GLORY's clinch rules leave a catch only a moment (FTX-DUTCH, para. beginning "Picture this"), so B's version is a check or a step, then the low kick to the landing leg [inference].
- He varies the combinations. Hoost warns: "never throw the same combination repeatedly ... because the opponent will see what is happening and easily counter us" (HOOST, section "Connecting Our strikes", para. 3) [observation]. Dekkers mixed "long powerful attacks with short, machine-gun-like rapid punches" and "often utilized long combinations consisting of more than 10 moves in a row" (BE, "Ramon Dekkers", introduction) [observation].
- His stance is "broader with a tighter, higher guard to slip into punches and absorb return fire", with "lateral movement, pivots off the hook, and aggressive step-ins behind the jab" (FTX-DUTCH, para. beginning "Muay Thai prefers a narrower stance") [observation].

### Ring use and tempo {#pressure-ring-and-tempo}

- He cuts the ring off rather than following. Teddy Atlas: "The first rule of ring cutting is not to follow the guy", because a followed opponent "has all the room in the world to move" and the follower runs into his punches (ATLAS, section "How To Cut Off The Ring", paras. 5–6) [observation]. The cutter stays "directly in front of your opponent", moves "parallel in front" of him and closes the space slowly: "if you do it too fast your opponent will notice" (ATLAS, same section, paras. 7–9) [observation]. Atlas is a boxing coach. The footwork transfers to the kickboxing ring unchanged, but a kicker can stop the advance with kicks at a distance a boxer cannot [inference].
- Coaches of the kicking arts name the same skill: "Cutting Off The Corners: Anticipating your opponent's movement and strategically positioning yourself to cut off their escape routes by angling your footwork" (EV-FOOT, section "Angles", last list item) [observation].
- K-1 pressure ends in the corner and on the ropes. Schilt began his 2005 Grand Prix by "pushing the Brazilian to the corner", and later "pushed Bonjasky into a corner, got a clinch" to land the finishing knees (SH-GP05, p. 1, paras. on the final and the semi-final) [observation]. Feitosa "drove his opponent back from the ropes at one side of the ring all the way to the other" with punches, kicks and a flying knee (SH-GP05, p. 1, Musashi semi-final) [observation].
- His tempo is high from the first bell. The early Dutch fighters' "intensity and work-rate was a key factor from the outset", against a Thai style that eases into a contest (ONE, para. beginning "The athletes traveling from the Netherlands") [observation]. "Dutch timing is more linear and sustained—jab to set range, pull a counter, blast the low kick. Feints happen, but the backbone is pressure and output" (FTX-DUTCH, para. beginning "Muay Thai plays with tempo") [observation]. Dutch kickboxing "evolved for three-round sprints where early damage secures the decision" (FTX-DUTCH, FAQ on pacing) [observation].
- Measured round-one intensity in K-1 rules: athletes "often want to terminate the fight quickly in the first round and therefore they engage in the fight in the first round to a maximal level" (RYDZIK, Discussion, p. 6) [observation]. See [round one's cost](#round-one-cost) for the heart rates.

### What it costs him {#pressure-costs}

- Pressure "uses lots of energy", carries "a high risk of eating hard counters on your way in" and "requires taking more damage" (EV-PP, section "Some Of The Disadvantages") [observation]. "The pressure style won't work for you if you gas easily" (EV-PP, section "Traits Of A Pressure Fighter") [observation].
- A pressure fighter has to plant to punch hard, and that is when low kicks find him. Against "good boxers or fighters who tend to throw long combinations", GLORY's coach advises staying at kicking distance to "chop down the legs. Kick them every time he tries to attack you with punches. He won't be able to block the kicks as he needs to plant his feet heavy on the ground if he wants to throw a strong punch", and the kicks throw him "off balance so he will not be able to hit you hard or even finish his combination" (GLK, para. beginning "An inside low kick is a strong weapon") [observation].
- A man coming forward behind a jab carries his weight on the front leg, where an outside low kick has "maximum effect" and "the opponent will not be able to block it on time" (GLK, para. beginning "Outside low kicks are usually thrown") [observation].
- A coach's account of a long-legged kicker against "a Dutch-style pressure fighter": "Round one, the pressure looked scary. By mid-round two, timed teeps and southpaw body kicks drained the gas tank" (FTX-DUTCH, para. beginning "One of my guys") [observation]. Translation: an anecdote, from a southpaw, over two rounds. In this film the drain must show within one round, so it can only begin to show by 150 s [inference].

### His typical weaknesses {#pressure-weaknesses}

- Following instead of cutting, he loses to the mover: "Eventually the fighter who is chasing loses the fight" (ATLAS, section "How To Cut Off The Ring", para. 2) [observation].
- Punching from a planted base, he cannot easily lift his weighted lead leg to check (GLK, as above; BE, "Rob Kaman", Technique #1, on weight on the lead foot) [inference from both].
- Committing to long combinations, he is predictable if he repeats them (HOOST, section "Connecting Our strikes", para. 3) [observation].
- Spending energy early, he faces a round in which his output and his guard must last (EV-PP, as above) [inference].
- For the brief: B's championship pressure is cut-off footwork, varied combinations and a high guard. His cost is breath, a planted lead leg in his combinations, and the counters and kicks that find him as he comes in [choice].

## The patient kicker and counter-fighter {#counter-kicker}

A is the challenger: a patient kicker with a check-and-counter game and right body kicks. Coaches call his pole of the spectrum the precision fighter.

### What he is {#kicker-profile}

- "The precision fighting style ... involves patiently waiting for openings that can be exploited with an accurate style. Precision fighters focus more on winning the cerebral aspects of a fight, using tools like feints, fakes, and traps to create openings" (EV-PP, section "Pressure Vs. Precision Fighting", para. 2) [observation]. He "thrive[s] under pressure (ironically). Countering aggressive opponents is your thing" (EV-PP, section "Traits Of A Precision Fighter") [observation].
- Translation: EV-PP writes for striking in general and names boxers and MMA fighters. The temperament and its trade-offs transfer to the kickboxer [inference], within one GLORY limit. GLORY counts as stalling both "Passivity; this includes but is not limited to fighting only when the opponent attacks" and "continuously pulling up the front leg or stretching out the front arm without carrying out a particular technique" (GLORY 5.07.C.1–2, p. 17) [observation]. A pure counter-fighter who waits for every attack, or a range-finding arm held out with no strike behind it, risks a caution (GLORY 5.08.A.1) [inference]. So the GLORY version of this temperament keeps its own initiative: it leads with kicks of its own between counters, and the touch that finds the range is a real strike ([range-finding](#kicker-repertoire)) [inference].

### Repertoire {#kicker-repertoire}

- The right body kick. Of hard body kicks, "Those things sting", so "it doesn't take much before your opponent starts dropping their hands to protect their body" (EV-QM, section 3 "Question Mark Kick Thrown Off Body Kicks") [observation]. The same body kick is the look from which the Brazilian kick is thrown (see [the Brazilian kick](#brazilian-kick)).
- The check and its counter (next section).
- Low kicks against the puncher, thrown "every time he tries to attack you with punches" from kicking distance (GLK, para. beginning "An inside low kick") [observation].
- Range-finding with the rear hand. Peter Aerts "would often use his right hand as a range-finder: if the right hand was near his opponents face, this was and indication that his right shin could reach the neck". In the technique shown, "Peter checks the distance by touching his opponent with his right cross as he pulls back and this hides his incoming right high kick" (BE, "Peter Aerts", Technique #3) [observation]. For an orthodox kicker this is the rear-hand cross, touching as it returns, not the lead hand [inference]. Hoost "often semi-connects with punches in order to feint or check the distance" (BE, "Ernesto Hoost", Technique #3) [observation].
- Timing the opponent's output. Brestovac's 36-second knockout of Hesdy Gerges at GLORY: "Gerges went to work with low kicks early on. Brestovac timed Gerges' output and then fired back a head kick" (GLORY-KKY, Brestovac vs Gerges) [observation].

### The check and counter {#check-and-counter}

- A check blocks a low or body kick with the shin. The defender lifts the leg, angles the shin outward and turns the hip, keeps the guard high, then returns the foot quickly: "Never leave it hanging" (EV-CHECK, sections "What Is A Check?" and "How To Check") [observation]. For a body kick "the leg must be lifted higher, with the shin positioned to protect the midsection" (EV-CHECK, FAQ "How Do I Check A Body Kick Versus A Low Kick?") [observation].
- The counter follows at once: "the right cross, which fires while the opponent is still recovering their kicking leg, and the inside low kick, which targets the supporting leg" (EV-CHECK, FAQ "What Should I Do Immediately After Checking A Kick?") [observation]. Under K-1 rules, "A check without an immediate return doesn't erase the damage. Build the habit: check, step, fire back now" (FTX-DUTCH, para. beginning "K‑1/Dutch: If the 1–2 lands clean") [observation].
- Checks wear the kicker down: "many fighters will visibly reduce their kicking output once they realise their shin is consistently meeting a hard check" (EV-CHECK, FAQ "Can Checking Kicks Hurt Your Opponent?") [observation]. GLORY's coach: "The pain the attacker feels will make him think twice about kicking again" (GLK, "Defending low kicks", item 2) [observation].
- Translation: EV-CHECK is Muay Thai. Under GLORY the check, its timing and its counters are the same. Only the catch-and-sweep counters are lost [inference, from the GLORY ban on throws and sweeps in the commission's ruleset].

### Angles, pivots and feints {#kicker-angles-feints}

- His footwork: "Pivoting: Rotating your lead foot or rear foot to change your angle"; "Stepping Off At 45-Degree Angles"; "Circling To Flank The Opponent"; "Lateral Shifting"; "V-Stepping" (EV-FOOT, section "Angles: Creating Openings And Neutralising Attacks", list) [observation].
- Against a man who comes forward, moving straight back is the error. Musashi "may have doomed himself by moving straight backwards when Feitosa charged, rather than moving off to the side" (SH-GP05, p. 1, Musashi semi-final) [observation].
- His feints: a feint is "any time you half-throw a technique that is not intended to land but to make the opponent flinch" (EV-FEINT, "What is a feint?") [observation]. Kicks are feinted by "a simple snap of the hip" with the feet planted, or by lifting the kicking foot, beginning the kick and putting it back down before kicking high (EV-FEINT, "Landing the head kick", para. beginning "What you want to do here") [observation]. The eyes feint too: look at the leg you pretend to attack (same section) [observation].

### How he answers pressure {#kicker-answers-pressure}

- Kick at range, land balanced, keep the guard high and leave on an angle: "Best answer: kick, land balanced, guard high, exit on angle—don't get stuck in the pocket" (FTX-DUTCH, para. beginning "K‑1/Dutch: Catching stops your momentum") [observation].
- Chop the puncher's planted legs and break his combinations (GLK, as in [his costs](#pressure-costs)) [observation].
- Drain him with body kicks over time (FTX-DUTCH anecdote, as in [his costs](#pressure-costs)) [observation].
- Precision "Frustrates aggressive opponents, leading to more openings" (EV-PP, section "Pros Of The Precision Fighting Style") [observation].

### Ring use {#kicker-ring-use}

- He circles and pivots off the line of the advance (EV-FOOT; SH-GP05, as above) [observation].
- He is in danger when his back reaches the ropes: the Sherdog reports of 2005 show K-1 pressure ending in corners and on the ropes (SH-GP05, p. 1) [observation]. The kicker can still kick there. Feitosa's knockdown of Carter Williams came "while along the ropes", with Williams on the ropes (SH-USA05, p. 1, Williams semi-final) [observation].
- For the brief: A gives ground on angles, not straight back. B's cut-offs are what push A toward the ropes, and the body kicks are thrown as B steps in [choice].

### His weaknesses {#kicker-weaknesses}

- "You need excellent distance management, speed, and reflexes"; the style "Works best for fighters who can strike while backpaddling"; "Close rounds could end up going to busier opponents" (EV-PP, section "Some Drawbacks Associated With The Out-Fighting Style") [observation].
- "Teeps and body kicks can leave you open to catches and grabs" (EV-BODY, section "The Risks When Targeting the Body in MMA") [observation]. Translation: in MMA a catch leads to a takedown. In GLORY it leads to a brief hold and a counter, so the risk is the counter as the kicker stands on one leg [inference].
- He gives ground and so concedes the look of control. Pressure is often misread as winning ("the fighter moving forward is the one that is winning", ATLAS, opening para.) [observation].
- For the brief: A's cost before 150 s is ground and the low kicks he eats when he cannot check because B's punches arrive first. His breath grows too, but less than B's ([round one's cost](#round-one-cost)) [choice].

## Championship level against club level {#level-markers}

Both men are championship level. Each marker below separates them from a club fighter. The installed package already carries measured timing, reaction, anticipation and guard-drop values from novice to elite (PKG, `StrikingSkill`), and they are not restudied here. This section records what the brief names in words.

### Guard discipline {#guard-discipline}

- A club fighter drops his hands. "Dropping The Hands: Always keep the guard high during a check" is a listed mistake (EV-CHECK, "Common Mistakes To Avoid") [observation]. Trainers should "program your fighters to keep their hands up after the bell sounds", because habits from the gym repeat in the ring (TITLE, para. beginning "Next, if you're the trainer") [observation].
- Even good fighters' hands drift when they think the head is safe: "All but the most disciplined fighters hands start to drop a little when they think their head is safe from attack" (EV-FEINT, "Landing the head kick", para. beginning "The effect of a continued attack") [observation]. So a championship fighter's guard can be conditioned too. That is the premise of B's tell [inference].

### Balance and return to stance {#balance-return}

- After a check, "return the leg quickly to your stance. Never leave it hanging" (EV-CHECK, "How To Check", "Land Firmly") [observation]. "Leaning Backward: This disrupts balance and prevents effective counters" (EV-CHECK, "Common Mistakes To Avoid") [observation].
- In a shin block, "Don't bend your torso ... You want to remain as upright as possible" (EV-BLOCK, section "1) The Shin Block") [observation].
- Fairtex's body-kick drill: "Score only when balance is perfect" (FTX-DUTCH, drill cues, para. beginning "Cues: Long hip turn") [observation].

### Economy and variety {#economy}

- A beginner learns each strike separately and later "stopping after each strike whats next"; a trained fighter's punches and kicks "complement each other" so he goes "smoothly through the moves" (HOOST, paras. beginning "Most practitioners don't practice" and "Once the practitioners work") [observation].
- The championship fighter does not throw the same combination repeatedly: "never throw the same combination repeatedly" (HOOST, section "Connecting Our strikes", para. 3) [observation]. The precision fighter asks "Why throw ten punches when one well-placed strike does the job?" (EV-PP, section "Traits Of A Precision Fighter") [observation].

### Defence while attacking {#defence-while-attacking}

- Each block is drilled together with its counter, "as a single sequence rather than two separate actions" (EV-BLOCK, FAQ "Why Does The Elbow Block Create A Counter Opportunity") [observation]. The check-and-counter is drilled "as a single unit" (EV-CHECK, FAQ "What Should I Do Immediately After Checking A Kick?") [observation].
- The guard stays high through the check (EV-CHECK, "How To Check", "Timing And Lift") [observation]. The right cross that touches to find the range also hides the right high kick that follows (BE, "Peter Aerts", Technique #3) [observation].
- The jaw stays shut on the mouthguard while exhaling, because with an open jaw "you are much more susceptible to being hurt from a counter strike" (MTG-GRUNT, para. beginning "First and foremost") [observation].

### Feints {#level-feints}

- Feinting and setting up "are probably two of the trickiest things to really learn in martial arts" (EV-FEINT, "Other feints and set up concepts", para. 1) [observation]. A feint only draws a real reaction once the opponent respects the real technique: "If you've already landed a hard leg kick ... your opponent knows to block it the next time" (EV-FEINT, "What is a setup?") [observation].
- At world-championship level feints are frequent enough to be coded as their own class. Ouergui's time-motion system for WAKO world championships classes "Feint" and "Deceptive movements (Foot, head/body, or hand feint [lead or rear])" as low-intensity activity (OUERGUI, Table 1, p. 3539) [observation]. Translation: WAKO full-contact is amateur, with 2-minute rounds and no low kicks. Only the existence of the feint class is adopted, not any rate [inference].

### Reading the opponent {#reading-the-opponent}

- Expert combat athletes "better anticipate the opponent's actions based on information perceived before and during the attack" (MQB19, Abstract) [observation]. In karate, experts predicted attacks above chance from the on-guard position alone, while near-experts needed to see the preparatory movement and the start of the attack (MQB19, p. 327, on Rosalie and Müller 2013) [observation].
- Experts fix their gaze "on a central and relatively still part of the opponent's body (i.e. head and trunk)", while less experienced fighters look at fists, arms or legs (MQB19, pp. 327–328, "Visual search behavior") [observation]. A coach's version: keep your gaze "somewhere at the opponents chest ... so that you can see the whole picture using your peripheral vision" (EV-FEINT, "Landing the head kick", para. beginning "One effective way") [observation].
- Elite checking reads "virtually every kick without conscious thought" and "is the product of years of accumulated sparring experience" (EV-CHECK, FAQ "How Long Does It Take To Develop Reliable Check Timing?") [observation].
- At world level, round one is spent reading: kickboxers spent the most low-intensity time (observation and preparation) in the first round, "probably used a strategy based on the observation of opponent's movements before initiating attacks" (OUERGUI, Discussion, p. 3542) [observation]. Translation: WAKO amateur full-contact. The qualitative pattern, reading early, fits a GLORY round one. The rates are left to the screenplay's study [inference].

### Breathing {#level-breathing}

- "Watch any experienced Muay Thai fighter closely. You'll notice a rhythm—a sharp exhale every time they throw a strike" (FTX-BREATH, section on the exhale) [observation]. Holding the breath during strikes or while being hit "tenses your body, drains your gas tank, and makes every shot hurt more" (FTX-BREATH, para. beginning "And worst of all?") [observation].
- Translation: Muay Thai coaching. Breathing on the strike is the same practice in kickboxing [inference].

### The level in numbers {#level-numbers}

- Fifteen elite amateur K-1 kickboxers measured during international bouts were 177.1 ± 4.8 cm (168–185 cm) tall and 79.1 ± 5.6 kg (67–86 kg) in mass. They had trained 9.9 ± 5.3 years (4–25 years), five to six times a week, with about 20 bouts each (RYDZIK, Table 1, p. 3, and "Characteristics of the Participants", p. 3) [measurement]. The commission's 180 cm and 80 kg men sit inside these ranges [inference].
- Translation: amateur K-1 league, three 2-minute rounds. Professional GLORY championship fighters are not measured here. A GLORY champion's training history lies at or above the measured upper quartile (11 years) [inference].
- For the brief: both men's cast records may give fifteen to twenty years of training and a professional record that reached a GLORY title fight [choice].

## How a tell forms and is exploited {#tell}

The commission's B learns, during this round, to drop his left arm (elbow to ribs) whenever A's right knee rises. A tests the habit with a feint and then finishes behind it.

### Why a guard drops to cover body kicks {#tell-formation}

- Body attacks pull the hands down. "Body shots can make opponents adopt defensive measures to protect their body, opening up opportunities for head shots" (EV-BODY, list of benefits) [observation]. With continued low and body attacks, "you'll notice your opponents guard start to relax and drop a little" (EV-FEINT, "Landing the head kick", para. beginning "The effect of a continued attack") [observation].
- The elbow block is a standard defence: "you bring your elbow downward to shield that side of your body. You can bend at the torso using this block" (EV-BLOCK, section "3) Elbow Block") [observation]. It also rewards the defender, since it "puts you in an excellent position to counter using the same arm" (same section) [observation]. Blocking kicks with the forearms is "something you should probably only do as a last resort" (EV-DEF, para. beginning "You can even block body and head kicks") [observation]. The preferred answer is the shin check (EV-DEF, para. beginning "Checking with your shins") [observation].
- The disciplined form keeps the head covered while the elbow covers the ribs: "Drill simultaneous high guard with elbow pin to ribs while lifting the shin" (FTX-DUTCH, FAQ on checking Dutch kicks) [observation].
- Blocks are drilled until each attack fires its block automatically, as a trained "stimulus-response pairing" (EV-BLOCK, FAQ "How Do I Build Blocking Habits That Work Under Pressure") [observation]. So an elite block is a fast, automatic answer to a cue. That makes it readable [inference].
- Why B uses his arm and not a check: B punches from a planted base (GLK). Weight on the lead foot makes a shin block of that leg difficult; in BE's example the weight comes from blocking a hook, and here it comes from B's own planted punching (BE, "Rob Kaman", Technique #1). A's right body kick reaches B's left side while that leg is loaded, so B covers with the left elbow. The block lets him counter with the same left arm, which is why he keeps doing it (EV-BLOCK). As he repeats it, the cover grows and the left glove leaves his head [inference, from the cited facts].
- For the brief: B's tell is the disciplined elbow block degraded under pressure. His left elbow drops to the ribs on the cue of A's rising right knee and takes the left glove down from the temple with it. Early in the round he covers correctly, with the glove still at the head [choice].

### Reading a tell in real fights {#tell-real-cases}

- At GLORY 45 in Amsterdam (30 September 2017), Mladen Brestovac stopped Hesdy Gerges with a head kick in 36 seconds (GLORY-KKY, Brestovac vs Gerges) [observation]. Brestovac afterwards: "The high kick was planned, it was part of the tactics, but we thought we would wait with it until the second or third round, when he tires and his guard weakens. But I saw that already at the start he lowers his hands to block the middle kick. So I just waited a little to set up the high kick" (INDEX, para. beginning "Nikad se ne obazirem"; this study's translation) [observation]. The GLORY sources disagree on the event: GLORY-KKY names GLORY 45 and dates the clip 30 September 2017, while GLORY-KKY-W names "GLORY 46 AMSTERDAM" (GLORY-KKY-W, list of other nominees) [observation].
- At the K-1 World Grand Prix 2005 Final event in Tokyo, Badr Hari knocked out Stefan Leko with a spinning back kick to the jaw: "you could see Leko drop his hands as he was backing out, perhaps thinking that the kick would come low" (SH-GP05, p. 2, Hari vs Leko) [observation].
- These cases show three things. A guard drawn low by expected body or low attacks is read and punished at K-1 and GLORY level. The reading can happen within the first minute. The finishing kick lands where the dropped hand used to be [inference].

### Testing the reaction with a feint {#tell-test}

- A feint asks a question and the reaction answers it: "All you're trying to do is get a reaction from your opponent" (EV-FEINT, "Other feints and set up concepts", para. 2) [observation].
- "A savvy opponent might feint just to get you to drop both hands towards your body before firing a powerful shot to your head" (EV-BLOCK, section "4) Double Forearm Block") [observation].
- Rob Kaman "feints an attack forcing Larry to lift his left leg for a shinblock" and kicks the foot as it lands; "Feinting kicks and attacking the defending leg was one of Kaman's favorite tactics", which works only after "you consistently 'tenderize' your opponent's thighs" (BE, "Rob Kaman", Technique #2) [observation].
- For the brief: the commission's "feint that lands" is read as a feint whose follow-up lands. A's right knee rises as for the body kick. B's elbow drops. A puts the foot down and lands a strike to the left side of B's head, where the glove has left. A strike to that side of the head proves the opening the finish will use. The follow-up strike is the brief's choice [choice].

### Setting up the high kick behind a body-kick look {#high-behind-body}

- Evolve sets out the head-kick plan in three conditions: the opponent is stationary, his guard is lowered, and he believes "a low line attack is coming instead". First land low and body kicks, which teaches him to block them, then fake the low attack and kick high (EV-FEINT, "Landing the head kick", list and following paras.) [observation].
- With the question-mark kick off body kicks: "Once your opponent starts blocking your body kicks, bring your knee up and pause for a second ... Redirect the kick once your opponent drops their guard" (EV-QM, section 3) [observation]. LK-QM agrees: repeated body kicks make the opponent assume more are coming and start "dropping their hands to block body kicks" (LK-QM, section "Question mark kick off body kicks") [observation].
- Translation: both are written for Muay Thai and MMA. The set-up is a sequence of legal GLORY strikes and transfers unchanged [inference].

### What anticipation research says {#anticipation-evidence}

- Experts use more than what they see. "Situational probabilities include the score, time to the end of the match and the habitual techniques of the opponent", and karate experts responded earlier when those probabilities made the coming attack predictable (MQB19, pp. 330–331, on Milazzo et al. 2016) [observation]. Coaches are advised to "obtain prior information from the opponent body and the environment ... more usual techniques of the opponent ... to estimate the probabilities of the opponent's actions" (MQB16, p. 13) [observation].
- The attacker is advised to exploit this: "minimize previous movement that can be used by the opponent to anticipate the subsequent attack or even make other movement that give a false impression about the impending attack" (MQB16, p. 13, "3. Application to training") [observation].
- How a kick reveals itself in time: "when the attacker lifts his/her leg from the floor and the knee is rising, the defender has information about the attacking leg but not where it will be directed. Later, during the leg extension, the defender knows the side of the body ... In the last moment of the extension, the defender knows if the kick is directed to the head or the trunk" (MQB19, p. 332) [observation].
- Together: a defender who commits to the body cover when the knee rises is acting on a learned probability, before the kick has shown its height. That is an expert's anticipation, and an attacker who has taught it can betray it [inference]. Translation: the review covers karate, taekwondo, boxing and fencing. The kick model is a roundhouse kick, which kickboxing shares [inference].
- Unavailable: the primary papers Milazzo, Farrow, Ruffault and Fournier (2016), Rosalie and Müller (2013), Mori, Ohtani and Imanaka (2002), and Mann, Schaefers and Cañal-Bruland (2014) were not retrieved. They are cited here only through MQB19 [observation].

## The Brazilian kick as an elite setup {#brazilian-kick}

The Brazilian or question-mark kick starts as a lower roundhouse or front-kick look and turns over at the top onto the head.

### What it is and where it comes from {#brazilian-what}

- "It starts like a regular low kick; then, you surprise your opponent by suddenly aiming higher halfway into the motion" (EV-QM, section "Understanding The Question Mark Kick") [observation]. "This motion of your leg looks just like a question, which is how the technique got its name" (LK-QM, section "How does the question mark kick work?") [observation].
- Its origin is disputed: probably karate, since it "uses a snapping motion, like many karate kicking techniques", while "Muay Thai fighters typically kick through their target" (EV-QM, intro, para. 3) [observation]. LK-QM also points to karate and names Saenchai among Thai users (LK-QM, section "Who created the question mark kick?") [observation].
- Glaube Feitosa, a Brazilian Kyokushin karateka who fought in K-1, "was known for his use of the 'question mark kick,' utilizing the technique in several knockout victories in his career" (WP-FEIT, lead) [observation].

### Why it beats a guard drawn low {#brazilian-why}

- The kick borrows a chamber the defender has learned to answer: "every single low kick or teep that comes could end up being turned into a knockout blow" (MTG-QM, para. beginning "This means") [observation].
- It goes over the lowered arm: "The kick goes up and over your opponent's guard" (EV-QM, section 3; LK-QM, section "Question mark kick off body kicks") [observation].
- A head kick need not land flush to finish. "A high kick doesn't even need to land completely flush in order to cause a knock down or even a knockout" (SKAR, para. 1) [observation]. The correct high-kick block flexes and tenses the arm on the kicked side, or holds it to the head "almost as if you are on the phone" (SKAR, paras. 4–6) [observation]. An arm dropped to the ribs is not there to do this [inference].
- Against B: his left elbow drops on the cue of the rising right knee. The same chamber then turns over, and the kick descends on the left side of his head, where the arm should be [inference, from MQB19 p. 332 and the above].

### What the chamber shows before it turns over {#brazilian-chamber}

- The thrower sells the lower kick. "Bring your knee up and pivot your front foot to open up your hips" (EV-QM, section 1). The knee comes up "high", the front foot turns "quickly to the side. This opens up your hips and enables you to rotate your kick over" (LK-QM, section "The back leg question mark kick") [observation].
- Coaches teach a pause with the knee up: "Take a slight pause and leave your knee up for half a second to sell the fake" (LK-QM, "Tips", item "Pause"). Evolve says "pause for a second" (EV-QM, section 3) [observation]. Translation: these are coaching cues for selling the fake in training. The elite kick's timing is left to the screenplay's study [inference].
- The difference is there for a trained eye: "there's a slight difference in the low kick's chamber and the question mark kick's. The knee from the kicking leg is clearly moving in a different direction. Your opponent may not catch onto this ... However, if they have a good enough eye, it can be seen" (MTG-QM, section "Deceiving your opponent with the teep") [observation].
- The one readable sign a source names is the knee's direction: "The knee from the kicking leg is clearly moving in a different direction" (MTG-QM, as above) [observation]. The thrower's own cues, a knee brought up high, a front foot pivoted to open the hips and the hips turning over (EV-QM; LK-QM), are what make that direction differ; that they are visible in their own right is not sourced [inference]. A defender who commits when the knee rises has already acted before the difference shows (MQB19, p. 332) [inference].
- For the brief: at championship level A hides the difference as far as he can. The camera, not B, may catch the knee travelling in a different direction from the body kick's. B has committed to his body cover before it does [choice].

### Knockouts it has produced {#brazilian-knockouts}

- Glaube Feitosa vs Carter Williams, K-1 World Grand Prix 2005 in Las Vegas, semi-final, 30 April 2005. Feitosa "was able to crash one of his tricky high kicks onto the head of Williams while along the ropes. The kick, where Feitosa brought his leg down in a sort of slingshot fashion, landed swiftly behind Williams' right ear, sending the one-time K-1 USA champion to the canvas". Williams rose at the count of nine, and Feitosa finished him later by axe kick and knee, a two-knockdown TKO at 2:56 of round two (SH-USA05, p. 1, paras. on the Williams semi-final) [observation]. Sherdog does not name the kick. It is taken as Feitosa's question-mark kick from its downward "slingshot" path and WP-FEIT's note on his signature technique [inference].
- Feitosa vs Gary Goodridge, the same night's final: "a crippling left high kick that crashed smack dab onto the right side of Goodridge's dome", stoppage at 2:40 of round one (SH-USA05, p. 1, paras. on the final) [observation]. WP-FEIT records it as "KO (left high kick)" and lists other left high-kick knockouts: Toa (2004), Ruslan Karaev (2006) and Alex Roberts (2008) (WP-FEIT, record table) [observation]. Neither source says which of these were question-mark kicks [observation].
- Israel Adesanya vs Stuart Dare, Hex Fight Series (MMA), first round: Adesanya "recorded a knockout victory with his now-iconic question mark kick" with seven seconds left in the round. At UFC 230 he "landed the iconic question mark kick and followed it up with strikes" for a first-round finish of Derek Brunson (SK-ADESANYA, paras. beginning "Israel Adesanya had great success", "In the fight against Dare" and "The Nigerian-New Zealander") [observation]. Translation: MMA, with small gloves and the threat of takedowns. Adesanya was also a GLORY kickboxer. The kick is the same striking action [inference].
- Chingiz Allazov vs Sitthichai Sitsongpeenong, ONE Featherweight Kickboxing World Grand Prix final, 2022: after a punch combination and an exchange of kicks Allazov "lands a question mark kick", explained as bringing "the knee up like they are striking the body, this is a misdirect, and then whips the foot higher". He won by decision (SK-ALLAZOV, paras. beginning "In the video" and "A question mark kick is") [observation]. So at elite kickboxing level the kick lands without always finishing [inference].
- No GLORY knockout by a question-mark kick was found in the retrieved GLORY sources. GLORY's 2017 knockout-kick nominees are head kicks of other kinds (GLORY-KKY) [observation].

### Where the coaching sources and the commission's landing differ {#brazilian-contact}

- Coaching texts land the question-mark kick on the chin with the ball of the foot and warn against the top of the foot: "Hit With The Ball Of Your Foot ... avoid hitting with the top of your foot to avoid an injury" (LK-QM, "Tips"; EV-QM, sections 1 and 3) [observation].
- The commission lands "instep and lower shin" on the side of the head and neck below and just behind the left ear. Feitosa's downward "slingshot" kick landed "behind Williams' right ear" (SH-USA05) [observation]. That a real elite kick landed there on a downward path supports the commission's landing site [inference].
- For the brief: keep the commission's landing. It is a downward roundhouse to the side of the head, as in the Feitosa case, not the karate snap to the chin. The contact surface and its law go to the screenplay's study [choice].

## What landed kicks cost during a round {#kick-costs}

The commission rules that blows before 150 s cost breath, guard position and balance, and that nobody goes down before the finish. This section gives the visible costs at the level a state record needs. It makes no injury claim, as the commission requires.

### Body kicks: breath, guard and posture {#body-kick-cost}

- Body attacks "Can take away an opponent's breath", are "Harder to recover from compared to head shots", and "can slow faster fighters down" (EV-BODY, list of benefits) [observation].
- Breath goes when the defender is not braced. "If you get hit when you don't expect it, that's when you get your breath knocked out", so a fighter breathes out to engage his abdomen as he is hit (FC, para. beginning "You have to breathe when you take a hit") [observation].
- A covering elbow bends the trunk: with the elbow block "You can bend at the torso" (EV-BLOCK, section "3) Elbow Block") [observation].
- The far end of the scale, which this film stops short of before 150 s: Schilt's "front kick to the mid-section" forced Bonjasky "down to one knee, wincing in pain" for an eight count, and a body knee "took the wind out of Bonjasky's sails" (SH-GP05, p. 1, semi-final) [observation]. Peter Aerts, who said he was kicked in the liver in the second round of a quarter-final, "was in obvious pain and had trouble leaving the interview space" afterwards (SH-GP05, p. 2, Aerts vs LeBanner) [observation].
- For the brief: B's state after each landed right body kick is a sharp audible exhale, a brief fold toward the kicked side, his left elbow clamping to the ribs, and a step or two in which he does not press. It is below a wince-and-kneel and never a knockdown. Each body kick that lands makes his next cover come sooner [choice].

### Low kicks: balance, stance and mobility {#low-kick-cost}

- "If you kick somebody off balance, it will disturb your opponent's attack". Enough thigh pain leaves the leg "temporarily 'paralyzed'", and legs "become feeling very heavy" (GLK, opening list and para. beginning "1. Outside low kick") [observation].
- A numb lead leg makes it "hard for them to judge movement and compromise[s] their balance", and reduces "their ability to move forward and unleash heavy strikes" (EV-LEG, section on the calf kick) [observation].
- Damage accumulates: fighters who do not check "pay for it in later rounds, when accumulated damage to the thigh begins to show in their footwork, their balance, and their ability to fight back" (EV-CHECK, "Final Thoughts") [observation]. Bonjasky's low kicks on Choi Hong-Man "eventually ... got through and staggered the Korean" (SH-GP05, p. 2) [observation].
- For the brief: A's state after B's landed low kicks is a lead foot that settles lighter, a stance that narrows or shortens, and a moment's shift of weight off the kicked leg before he moves again. It is never a fall [choice]. B's state after A's checks is the same in miniature, on his kicking shin, and he throws fewer low kicks once checks meet them (EV-CHECK) [inference].

### Round one's cost in breath {#round-one-cost}

- After the first 2-minute round of international amateur K-1 bouts, the heart rate of fifteen elite kickboxers was 178.2 ± 5.5 bpm (172–190 bpm), against 97.5 ± 5.6 bpm after the warm-up. Blood lactate was 11.3 ± 1.4 mmol/L (8.5–13.9 mmol/L), against 2.2 ± 0.9 mmol/L. Both were measured in the 1-minute break by chest strap and finger-stick sample (RYDZIK, Tables 2 and 3, pp. 3–4, and "Physiological Measurements", p. 4) [measurement].
- Translation: amateur K-1, 2-minute rounds. At 150 s of a 3-minute GLORY round both men have worked longer than those 120 s. Their load is therefore at least as high, and both are breathing hard and audibly by the finish [inference].
- For the brief: both men's breath grows across the round. The man who has taken body kicks, B, breathes harder and in shorter breaths. The man who has given ground, A, shows his cost in a narrowing stance rather than in breath [choice].

## Conduct of championship fighters {#conduct}

The procedure of the start (instructions, bell, "Fight") is in the [rules study](rules.md#round-start). This section records the fighters' own conduct.

### The glove touch {#glove-touch}

- "Touching gloves is the way in most combat sports to show respect before a fight" (EV-ETIQ, section on respect, para. 2) [observation].
- A boxing trainer would have fighters touch only "when the referee tells you to", after the instructions and before the final round. He warns against the habit of dropping the hands at the bell: "keep their hands up and their eyes on their opponent until the action has clearly stopped" (TITLE, paras. beginning "First of all" and "Next, if you're the trainer") [observation].
- Striking during the touch breaks an "age-old gentleman's agreement": at WBK 22 in China, Ibragim Khalilov knocked out Bakhtiyar Barotov "off a customary touching of gloves" three seconds into the fight, a "cheap shot" (SCORE, paras. 1–3) [observation]. Translation: an MMA event. The custom is the same in kickboxing [inference].
- For the brief: both men step in and touch gloves openly. The first exchange starts only once the gloves have parted and both are back in guard, a beat later, from the man who wants it. A is not being ambushed and neither man fakes the touch [choice].

### Silence {#silence}

- GLORY lists "spitting, cursing, talking, making rude sounds or obscene gestures" as unsportsmanlike conduct (GLORY 5.07.D.1, p. 17) [observation]. Every athlete "must wear a mouthguard during the Match" (GLORY 4.01.E, p. 5) [observation].
- Fighters exhale "while simultaneously biting down on your mouth guard" (MTG-GRUNT, para. beginning "First and foremost") [observation].
- So championship fighters do not speak in the round. Their voices are breath and effort sounds made through closed teeth on the mouthguard [inference].

### Exhale sounds on strikes {#exhale-sounds}

- The exhale is "a short, sharp burst of breath", heard as "'hiss' or 'hup!' sounds during sparring or fights" (FTX-BREATH, sections on the exhale and the sound) [observation]. "You're not going to make a sound on your inhale ... It's on the exhale" (FC, para. beginning "You're not going to make") [observation].
- The sound sits with the impact: "Right when you engage the bag is exactly the moment when you let out some noise" (FC, para. beginning "I don't tense up") [observation]. A kick's exhale is longer, because "it takes a lot of energy to lift that leg high" (FC, para. beginning "If you're throwing a kick") [observation].
- Translation: Muay Thai and fitness-boxing coaching. The exhale is the same in a GLORY ring [inference].
- For the brief: each strike carries a short sharp exhale through the teeth, a kick a slightly longer one. None is a shout [choice].

### Corner instructions during the round {#corner-instructions}

- In GLORY, "During the Match, Cornermen must sit in their designated seats and remain seated. Excessive coaching, entering the Ring at any time during the Match ... and pounding on the mat or apron of the Ring is prohibited" (GLORY 5.03.B, p. 10) [observation]. Coaching from the seats is therefore allowed in moderation [inference].
- A trained fighter takes instructions without looking: fighters should "listen to instructions and get advice without stopping the action or, worse yet, stopping to look at their coach" (TITLE, para. beginning "You should also train") [observation].
- This conflicts with the commission. Its language ruling makes "the referee's words the only speech" (commission, [language](../../instructions/commission.md#language)) [observation]. For the brief: the corner teams sit outside the ropes and coach by gesture, or are heard only as wordless calls under the crowd. The fighters never turn to them [choice].

### Respect {#respect}

- "At all times during the Match, Athletes must conduct themselves in a sportsmanlike manner and follow the instructions of the Referee and Officials" (GLORY 5.02.E, p. 10) [observation].
- For the brief: no taunting, no showboating and no protest at the referee. Respect shows at the touch and in obeying every break [choice].
