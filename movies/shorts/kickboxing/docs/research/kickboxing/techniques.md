# Kickboxing technique at championship level

The laws of the techniques the round shows: stance and footwork, punches, kicks, the clinch knee, defences, feints, and the right Brazilian kick, with the outcome table their exchanges can produce and the markers of the depicted level. The production has no motion vocabulary installed, so every technique the screenplay names is studied here. Sources and locators are in the [research index](../index.md).

Observation kinds: *measured* for a reported measurement, *coaching* for a coaching text's description, *rule* for rule text, *inference* for the production's reading of those, *choice* for the production's own decision. Left and right are given for an orthodox fighter, left foot and left hand forward.

## Stance and footwork

### Stance {#stance}

- The body stands about 45 degrees side-on, feet about shoulder width apart, weight even on both feet, knees slightly bent, lead toes turned slightly in, rear heel slightly raised, lead hand at eye level, chin down behind the lead shoulder, rear elbow close to the ribs, rear fist near the chin (AIBA Coaches Manual 2.5.1). *coaching*
- Feet too wide hinder movement, too narrow lose balance, flat heels slow the feet, an upright trunk enlarges the target and a raised chin exposes it (AIBA 2.5.1, common mistakes). *coaching*
- Kickboxers who kick weight the balls of the feet so either leg can leave the floor (Evolve, roundhouse kick, step 2). *coaching*

### Steps, pivots and circling {#footwork}

- Every step moves the foot nearest the direction of travel first and restores stance width after it: forward, lead foot then rear; backward, rear foot then lead; to the left, the lead foot first for an orthodox fighter; to the right, the rear foot first (AIBA 2.5.2). Weight stays on both legs and never on the heels. *coaching*
- A step back defends against any basic punch; a jump back moves both feet together and lands in stance (AIBA 2.5.4, stepping back and jumping back). *coaching*
- Against an opposite-stance or heavy-handed opponent the defender keeps moving to both sides and circles away from the dominant hand; on the ropes or in a corner he counters with sidesteps to either side and uses feints to draw the opponent in before stepping out (AIBA 2.7.1 and 2.7.2). *coaching*
- Dutch-style fighters often stay light on their feet, hopping in and out of range so that they are within reach only when they start the action (Bloody Elbow, Dutch Muay Thai breakdown, Souwer example). *coaching*

## Punches

### Straight punches, hooks and uppercuts {#punches}

- Each punch starts and ends in stance, travels out and back along the same path, and keeps the other arm up and rigid to guard head and body (AIBA 2.5.3, lead and rear uppercut to the body, steps 5 and 8). *coaching*
- The body uppercut bends the knees, turns the trunk towards the punching side, shifts weight onto the leg on that side and drives the fist forward and slightly upward with the elbow bent (AIBA 2.5.3). The rear-hand punch shifts weight from the rear leg to the lead leg as it lands. *coaching*
- Combinations start and finish with the lead hand where possible, and two- and three-punch combinations are thrown standing or with a step forward or back, pivoting hips and shoulders between punches (AIBA 2.5.5 and 2.7). *coaching*
- Range decides the punch: at long range a step forward is needed and straight punches dominate; at medium range straights without full extension and hooks land without stepping; at short range, gloves nearly touching, only short hooks and uppercuts fit (AIBA 2.5.7). *coaching*

## Kicks

### The rear-leg roundhouse kick {#roundhouse}

- Phases: preparation from the support foot's toe-down to the kicking foot's toe-off; chamber until the kicking knee starts to extend; extension until contact; recoil from contact until the kicking heel is down again (Gavagan and Sayers 2017, methods). *measured*
- The lead foot steps out at about 45 degrees and the fighter rises onto its ball for the whole kick, turning the hip over and swinging the kicking-side arm down in one motion, then returns the leg along its path to stance (Evolve, roundhouse kick, steps 1 to 5). *coaching*
- Contact is made with the instep and the front of the shin (Gavagan and Sayers 2017 define the contact area as the top of the foot to the mid-front shin). Low kicks may land with the lower shin, for range, or the upper shin, for damage (Evolve University, Dutch vs Thai kick). *measured*, *coaching*
- The Dutch low kick has little chamber and an almost straight leg rising at about 45 degrees, its rotation driven more by the shoulders than the hips; it is faster and retracts sooner than the Thai low kick, which steps out and turns the hip fully (Evolve University, Dutch vs Thai kick). *coaching*
- A body kick that lands with a straight leg strikes the side of the trunk; with a bent leg it strikes further forward (Evolve University, Buakaw example). *coaching*

### The front kick and the teep {#teep}

- The teep, a push with the sole of the foot, holds an opponent outside punching range; with a slight lean of the head it keeps the kicker out of reach (Bloody Elbow, Verhoeven example). GLORY forbids linear kicks to the legs (GLORY 5.07 A4), so the teep targets the trunk. *coaching*, *rule*

### The clinch knee {#clinch-knee}

- The clinch is legal only to land one immediate knee, after which the fighters disengage at once (GLORY 5.07 A11). *rule*
- In the Dutch style the knee is used less from the clinch than as an outside weapon (Bloody Elbow, Groenhart example). *coaching*
- Within the rule the production stages the clinch knee as one hand or both hands catching the back of the opponent's neck, the knee of the rear leg driving up into the body, and both men releasing as it lands. *choice*

### The right Brazilian kick {#brazilian-kick}

- The question-mark kick, also called the Brazilian kick, starts like a lower kick and is redirected upward halfway, so the leg traces a question mark; it snaps like a karate kick rather than kicking through the target (Evolve, question mark kick). *coaching*
- Thrown off body kicks: after hard roundhouse kicks to the body make the opponent drop his hands to protect it, the kicker raises the knee as for another body kick and pauses for an instant, then redirects the kick up and over the dropped guard (Evolve, question mark kick, variation 3). With the rear leg the lead foot pivots to open the hips as the knee rises (variation 1). *coaching*
- Head kicks thrown once the opponent's attention and defences are drawn low are a recurring Dutch pattern (Bloody Elbow, Hoost example). *coaching*
- The coaching text lands the kick with the ball of the foot on the chin. The commission lands it with the instep and lower shin coming down over the lowered arm onto the side of the head and neck. The production's kick therefore turns the hip over further than the coaching variant, so the shin and instep descend onto the target instead of the ball of the foot rising into it. This is the production's departure from the studied analogue. *choice*
- The snap matters to its timing: karate roundhouse kicks show less knee flexion at impact and faster knee extension than Muay Thai kicks (Gavagan and Sayers 2017, table 2). *measured*

### Measured kick targets {#kick-targets}

From 24 highly skilled practitioners kicking a pad set at mastoid height, three kicks each (Gavagan and Sayers 2017, table 2 and results). Values are mean ± standard deviation. The pad was fixed and unopposed, so values against a moving opponent are an approximation. *measured*

| Measure | Muay Thai | Karate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Execution time, preparation to heel-down | 1.02 ± 0.15 s | 1.29 ± 0.28 s |
| Extension phase | 0.074 ± 0.018 s | 0.087 ± 0.073 s |
| Recoil phase | 0.65 ± 0.17 s | 0.85 ± 0.31 s |
| Knee flexion at impact | 43 ± 7° | 15 ± 6° |
| Maximum knee extension velocity | 706 ± 200 °/s | 947 ± 94 °/s |
| Pelvis forward axial rotation | 92 ± 23° | 114 ± 30° |
| Linear foot velocity at impact | 7.22 ± 1.47 m/s | 5.57 ± 0.80 m/s |

Across disciplines the phases share the execution time as preparation 6%, chamber 14%, extension 11% and recoil 69% (Gavagan and Sayers 2017, results). The chamber, the moment a defender reads a rising knee, therefore lasts on the order of 0.15 s. *measured*, *inference*

## Defences

### Blocks and covers {#blocks}

- Double-arm cover: elbows and fists together, chin down, arms rigid, against straight punches to head and body (AIBA 2.5.4). *coaching*
- Catch: the open rear glove placed just in front of the chin, held rigid so it is not driven into the face (AIBA 2.5.4). *coaching*
- Rear-arm block: the trunk turns slightly to the lead side and the rear forearm rises rigid beside the head against the lead hook (AIBA 2.5.4). *coaching*
- Elbow block: the trunk turns to bring the forearm and elbow over the struck side of the body, against straight punches, hooks and uppercuts to the body (AIBA 2.5.4). The same elbow drop covers the ribs against a body kick on that side. *coaching*, *inference*
- Shoulder block: weight back, chin behind the lead shoulder, lead elbow tight to the ribs, rear arm high by the chin, against the rear straight to the head (AIBA 2.5.4). *coaching*

### Parries, slips and rolls {#evasions}

- Parry: the trunk turns slightly to the lead side and the edge of the rear hand deflects the opponent's lead straight without reaching forward or crossing the face (AIBA 2.5.4, parry with rear arm). *coaching*
- Slip: knees bend and the trunk turns so the head leaves the punch's line to one side, eyes on the opponent, forearms and elbows guarding; to the lead side against the rear straight, to the rear side against straights and hooks (AIBA 2.5.4, ducking to the lead and rear side). *coaching*
- Roll: the knees bend, the upper body moves towards the incoming hook and then the other way as the punch passes over the head, and the fighter stands back into stance (AIBA 2.5.4, rotation). *coaching*
- Sway: weight moves to the rear leg and only the upper body leans back, both arms up (AIBA 2.5.4, swaying back). *coaching*
- Common defensive faults are closing the eyes, defending too early or too late, defending without cover for the next punch, moving too wide, low or far, stiffness, and not returning to stance (AIBA 2.5.4). *coaching*

### The check {#check}

- The defender lifts the lead leg, turns the shin outward with a slight hip rotation and meets the kick with the upper shin or knee before it reaches the target, keeping the guard high; against a body kick the leg lifts higher to cover the midsection; the foot then returns to the floor at once (Evolve, how to check kicks). *coaching*
- A check is an opening: the right cross while the kicker is still recovering the leg, or the inside low kick to his support leg as his weight shifts, should follow without a pause (Evolve, how to check kicks). *coaching*
- A clean check hurts the kicker's shin, and repeated checks make fighters kick less (Evolve, how to check kicks). *coaching*

## Feints {#feints}

- A feint is a fake movement of hands, body, legs or head meant to trick the opponent; it prepares an attack, comes before it, may be made moving forward, back or sideways, and must be quick, with a quick action after it (AIBA 2.5.6). Examples are a half-extended lead hand and a flex of the lead knee that pretends a step (AIBA 2.5.6). *coaching*
- Against a counter-fighter, feints draw him into leading so that his lead can be countered (AIBA 2.7.1). *coaching*
- A feint of a rising rear knee works only against a defender who answers the knee before he sees the kick's height; it has no effect on one who waits. *inference*

## Outcome table {#outcomes}

Each row pairs the intents of attacker and defender with the outcome classes the sources permit. A landed strike's cost is breath for a body blow, balance and a moment of weight off the leg for a low kick, and guard position and balance for a glancing head blow.

| Attacker's intent | Defender's intent or state | Permitted outcomes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Punch or kick from correct range | No defence in time | Lands clean, costing the defender by target |
| Punch or kick from correct range | The matching block, parry, slip, roll, sway or step back, in time | No contact on target; the defender's counter window opens |
| Punch or kick from correct range | The matching defence, late | Glancing contact on the guard or target |
| Punch or kick from wrong range | Any | Falls short or is smothered |
| Low kick | Check in time | Shin meets shin; the kicker's balance and shin pay; the checker counters with the cross or the inside low kick |
| Combination ending in a low kick | Opponent backing out of punch range | The kick reaches him at its longer range and the kicker leaves punch range as it lands (Bloody Elbow) |
| Rear body kick | Lead elbow dropped to the ribs | Lands on the arm and ribs; breath and arm pay less than an unguarded rib shot |
| Feint the defender answers | The answered defence moves a guard off a target | The follow-up on the vacated target lands |
| Feint the defender ignores | Defender waits | Nothing; the feinter may be countered |
| Clinch with one immediate knee | Any | The knee lands or is blocked by the forearms; both release at once |
| Clinch held without a knee | Any | Foul; the referee breaks |
| Brazilian kick from the body-kick chamber | Guard dropped low on the kicked side | The shin and instep land on the side of the head: knockdown, at full power a knockout ([fall law](#knockout-fall)) |
| Brazilian kick from the body-kick chamber | Guard high | Blocked on the forearm; the kicker recoils exposed |
| Body blows before the finish | Any | Breath, guard and balance cost; no knockdown (commission) |

### The knockout fall {#knockout-fall}

- In 153 boxing and MMA knockouts, 134 (87.6%) showed an immediate near-total loss of muscle tone after a single head blow, followed by a free fall to the canvas (Huang et al. 2023, table 2). *measured*
- Of those 134, one fighter reflexively stepped and still fell; none of the rest moved the legs during the fall. 26 (19.4%) partly braced the fall with one or both arms; in boxing knockouts alone 2 of 26 (7.7%) did (Huang et al. 2023, pages 4 and 6). *measured*
- The fallen fighters were on the canvas within about a second; free-fall times are about 450 ms for the hips, from about 1 m, and 640 ms for the head, from about 2 m; falls went in every direction (Huang et al. 2023, page 4). *measured*
- No facial expression accompanied head knockouts; afterwards the fighter on the floor typically appears dazed and weak, the loss of tone recovering within about a second at its shortest (Huang et al. 2023, pages 3, 5 and 6). *measured*
- Knockouts from body blows took 2 to 3 seconds to reach the floor, with steps and bracing, never a free fall (Huang et al. 2023, page 4). This is why the commission's body blows cost breath and guard but drop nobody before the finish. *measured*, *inference*
- A fighter struck on the left side of the head with his weight carried left falls away from the blow, onto his right side. *inference*

## Markers of the depicted level {#level-markers}

Championship fighters show none of the beginner faults the coaching texts list: feet that cross or square up, flat heels, a raised chin, defences made too early or too late or without cover for the next blow, punches that do not return along their path, and failure to return to stance (AIBA 2.5.1, 2.5.2 and 2.5.4). *coaching* They chain technique into combinations and follow a check or a slip with a counter at once (AIBA 2.5.5; Evolve, how to check kicks). *coaching* Their kick timing is that of the measured experts above. *measured*
