# How a championship round unfolds

The time structure, tempo, spatial strategy and pattern-building of a high-level kickboxing round, for a screenplay that must fill 150 seconds of fighting with a changing contest rather than one repeated exchange. Technique laws are in the [technique study](techniques.md); sources and locators are in the [research index](../index.md).

Observation kinds: *measured*, *coaching*, *rule*, *inference*, *choice*, as in the technique study.

## Time structure {#time-structure}

From 45 bouts, 135 rounds, of two WAKO world championships, two-minute rounds, analysed by one observer at an intraclass reliability of 0.99 to 1 (Ouergui et al. 2014, tables 2 to 4). Values are mean ± standard deviation. *measured*

| Measure | All rounds | First rounds |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One high-intensity action: attack or defence until the last limb returns | 2.2 ± 1.2 s | 2.1 ± 1.2 s |
| One low-intensity action: footwork, stance, feints | 2.2 ± 0.7 s | 2.4 ± 0.8 s |
| Interval between consecutive high-intensity actions | 3.4 ± 1.2 s | 3.2 ± 1.2 s |
| Referee pause | 5.4 ± 4.5 s | 5.1 ± 4.3 s |
| High-intensity actions per round | | 27.1 ± 7.1 |
| Total high-intensity time per round | | 51.6 ± 17.0 s |
| Total low-intensity time per round | | 65.1 ± 10.2 s |
| Total pause time per round | | 12.8 ± 11.4 s |

- Fighting and non-fighting time stand at about 1:1, roughly 40% of a round in exchanges and 60% in preparation and pauses, in every weight class and round (Ouergui et al. 2014, discussion). *measured*
- First rounds have the most low-intensity time and the fewest pauses: fighters observe the opponent before committing; later rounds bring longer combinations, more clinches and more referee breaks (Ouergui et al. 2014, discussion). *measured*
- Translation to this bout: the study's bouts are amateur full-contact rounds of two minutes; this bout is a professional three-minute round under GLORY rules with low kicks and the clinch knee. Holding the measured rate, the 150 seconds before the finish hold about 26 to 43 exchanges, the first-round count of 27.1 ± 7.1 per 120 seconds scaled to 150, of about two seconds each, separated by about three seconds of footwork and feints, with few referee pauses. The rate is an approximation to be checked against professional footage, which is unavailable here. *inference*

## Styles and their patterns {#styles}

- The Dutch style, the kickboxing of K-1 and GLORY, relies on punches far more than Thai boxing does, opens its bursts with punch combinations and ends them with a low kick: the kick needs the punches to hide it from a check, hurts the leg and takes mobility, and at its longer range carries the kicker out of punching distance as it lands (Bloody Elbow, Dutch Muay Thai breakdown). *coaching*
- Dutch low kicks are thrown before or after a boxing combination: a low kick draws attention to the legs, punches follow, and when the opponent shells up the kicker returns to the legs (Evolve University, Dutch vs Thai kick). *coaching*
- Some Dutch fighters defend by moving out of range and teeping rather than checking; others stand and trade, accepting blows to land their own (Bloody Elbow, Souwer, Verhoeven and van Roosmalen examples). *coaching*
- Head kicks follow once attention and defences have been drawn low (Bloody Elbow, Hoost example). Body kicks that hurt make the opponent drop his hands to protect the body, which sets up the Brazilian kick from the same knee lift (Evolve, question mark kick, variation 3). *coaching*

## Spatial strategy {#space}

- Long range is preferred by tall fighters and usually happens in the centre of the ring; medium range demands constant movement; short range belongs to strong, short fighters (AIBA 2.5.7). *coaching*
- Against a heavy puncher, keep moving, make it hard for him to set his feet, and move around the ring in all directions instead of exchanging; against a counter-fighter, feint to make him lead, then counter (AIBA 2.7.1). *coaching*
- A fighter trapped on the ropes or in a corner escapes by changing places with the attacker, countering with sidesteps, and drawing him in with feints before stepping out (AIBA 2.7.2). *coaching*
- In a ring 7.00 m square (GLORY 4.06 B), two fighters at kicking range occupy a band of a few metres, so a fighter who gives ground straight back reaches the ropes within a few steps; circling and pivots keep him in the open ring. *inference*

## Pattern-building and tells {#tells}

- A defender who has been hurt by a technique starts defending it early: the opponent who starts checking low kicks can be hit high, and the opponent who has absorbed hard body kicks starts dropping his hands at the next knee lift (Evolve, question mark kick, variations 3 and 5). *coaching*
- The deceiver holds the knee at the top of the chamber for an instant so the defender commits to the body defence before the kick's height is decided (Evolve, question mark kick, variation 3). *coaching*
- At about 0.15 s, the measured chamber is shorter than a deliberate reading of the kick's height, so a defender who has learned to answer the rising knee answers before he could see where the kick goes. *inference* from the [kick targets](techniques.md#kick-targets).

## Conduct and level {#conduct}

- The fighters do not talk; talking and rude sounds are unsportsmanlike (GLORY 5.07 D1). *rule*
- Touching gloves at the start of the fight is a mark of respect the referee orders with "shake hands" in WAKO (WAKO Referee Rules 3.4.4). *cross-check*
- At this level every attack is a combination or a set-up, every defence returns to stance and leads to a counter, and the round changes as each man learns the other (AIBA 2.5.5 and 2.7; Ouergui et al. 2014, discussion). *coaching*, *measured*
