# Ring craft

This study records how championship fighters use the ring between and during exchanges: angles, cutting off, the ropes, exits, stance and range, and the targets a composed round must meet. Single footwork steps are performed by the installed `step` action, which carries its own study; this study supplies the strategy that chooses them. Each observation carries its kind as the [index](../index.md#observation-kinds) defines it; source ids are in the [index](../index.md#sources).

## Angles and lateral movement {#movements}

- [reported] "You don't want to move straight back to get away from an opponent's offense, and you don't want to attack straight down the middle too often as well"; "coaches will always instruct their students to move off the centerline, entering and exiting range by using angles" (`EVOLVE-GENERALSHIP`, §2).
- [reported] "Lateral movement can be used to evade strikes, create distance, and set traps"; "Pivoting: Rotating your lead foot or rear foot to change your angle of attack or defence"; "Stepping Off At 45-Degree Angles: Moving diagonally" (`EVOLVE-FOOTWORK`).
- [reported] "Your lead foot must constantly be on the opposite side of your opponent's lead foot, with the ability to pivot away from an opponent's power hand … you can easily circle away from danger when needed" (`EVOLVE-GENERALSHIP`, §3).
- [reported] "One common mistake that aggressive offensive fighters make is that they try to chase their opponents around the ring" (`EVOLVE-GENERALSHIP`, §4).

Law [inference]: fighters move off the line between them rather than straight forward or back. Between two orthodox men the power hand is the right, so a fighter who wants out of danger circles toward his own left, away from the other man's right. A pivot on the lead foot turns a fighter a quarter circle around his opponent and puts him on a new line.

## Cutting off the ring {#cut-off}

- [reported] "The first rule of ring cutting is not to follow the guy" (`DS-ATLAS-CUTOFF`); "do not follow the fighter moving outside on the same tracks"; "you move sideways like the linebacker and little by little you shrink the ring"; "you close the distance a little bit but be alert because they might stop and punch. You can't just walk in there" (`DS-ATLAS-LATERAL`).
- [reported] "If your opponent takes a step to their left, you mirror their lateral movement by taking a step to your right … This eventually leads to them being trapped along the ropes"; "herd your opponent into a corner" (`EVOLVE-CUTOFF`, §2–3); "If he goes left, you go left" (`EXPERTBOXING-CUTOFF`, §2), the same movement in the cutter's own frame.
- [reported] The ring shrinks "from its full size, to a half of its size, to a quarter"; "if the ring cutter steps too deeply to obstruct his opponent's path, the opponent will be free to escape out the other side" (`FP-CUTTING`).
- [reported] "Don't jump into the corner with your opponent, maintain some space to keep him in the corner" (`EXPERTBOXING-CUTOFF`, §3).

Law [inference]: the cutter steps sideways to stay square in front of the circling man, shortening his escape on each side, and closes only a little at a time, keeping enough space that his opponent cannot turn him. The pressure ends with the opponent's back near the ropes or a corner and the cutter one step outside striking range.

## The ropes {#ropes}

- [reported] "The fighter along the ropes is given three choices—move left, move right, or stand and fight"; "the trick that will carry a fighter out of bad spots even against the world's best ring cutters is the direction change. Simply side stepping one way, driving off the leading foot, and going back the other way" (`FP-CUTTING`).
- [reported] "The lesson hammered into young boxers is that the ropes are on fire … his rear ankle will often graze the ropes before he is truly put against them" (`FP-CUTTING`).
- [reported] "Never take a back-step … Go forward, sideways, or circle around him" (`EXPERTBOXING-CUTOFF`, avoiding the ropes).
- [reported] A cutter who "pivots around his lead foot and follows" gives the circler "space … to follow the ropes and then move back out into centre ring" (`FP-CUTTING`).

Law [inference]: a fighter whose rear foot reaches the ropes does not back further; he steps one way along them and drives back the other way, or stands and fights. The cutter who follows him around instead of mirroring lets him back to the centre.

## Leaving after a combination {#exit}

- [reported] "A good rule of thumb is to step to the side you finish on. So, if you finish with your lead hand, step to your lead side. If you finish with your rear hand, exit to your rear side … the pivot step to the lead side and the slide step to our rear side. Get off the tracks!" (`EASTON-12516`, `EASTON-62016`, the same paragraph).
- [reported] "Try slipping after every combination you throw"; the fighter "resets to his orthodox stance with a high guard" (`FIGHTERSONLY-38168`, free portion).

Law [inference]: after his combination a fighter leaves the line toward the side his last strike came from, pivoting to the lead side or sliding to the rear side, guard high. A cutter who is driving his opponent to the ropes keeps the line instead, so as not to give back the ground.

## Stance and stepping {#stance}

- [reported] Knees "slightly bent" (`ONEFC-STANCE`), "try not to stand straight up" (`LOWKICK-STANCE`); feet "a little more than shoulder-width apart" (`ONEFC-STANCE`) or "at or slightly smaller than shoulder-width" (`LOWKICK-STANCE`); weight "evenly distributed over both legs" (`ONEFC-STANCE`; `IBA` p. 34).
- [reported] "You must keep your feet light"; "heels are slightly up with your weight on the soles of your feet" (`LOWKICK-STANCE`); in Muay Thai "rarely do you see an athlete bouncing around on the balls of their feet" (`ONEFC-STANCE`).
- [reported] "The foot which stands closest to the direction of the movement starts moving first", the other following (`IBA` pp. 36–39); "never let your feet touch or cross while in your stance" (`LOWKICK-STANCE`).
- [reported] The lead hand and lead foot "measure the distance to the opponent" (`IBA` p. 123); "jabs to read your opponent" (`EVOLVEU-RANGE`).

Law [inference]: both fighters stand orthodox, knees bent, feet about shoulder width, weight even and on the soles, and step with the foot nearest the direction of travel first, never crossing. Between exchanges the lead glove reaches out to read the distance.

## Range and footwork volume {#range}

- [reported] Long range needs a step to land: "these techniques require the attacker to take a step forward"; mid range is where punches, knees and kicks "exchange attacks"; close range is hooks, uppercuts and the clinch (`EVOLVEU-RANGE`). "Long distance is the range that the distance from the opponent is far enough that the boxer cannot deliver the punches without stepping forward" and "long range boxing usually occurs at the center of the ring" (`IBA` p. 71).
- [reported] In elite amateur boxing a fighter spent 64.0 ± 22.3 s stepping and 57.3 ± 27.3 s bouncing in a first round of 180 s; "winners bounce more and step less than losers" (`DUNN-2017`, Table 3, pp. 4–6).
- [reported] No source located measures footwork steps per minute in kickboxing, boxing or Muay Thai competition. Walking at 100 to 119 steps a minute is "brisk" and 120 or more covers "all faster forms of locomotion" (`TUDOR-LOCKE-2018`, p. 776), a walking band, not a fighting one.

Law [inference]: between exchanges the fighters stand at long range, a step outside each other's reach, and their feet keep moving for most of the round; an exchange begins with the step that brings the attacker into mid range, and ends with one fighter stepping back out or off the line.

## Measurable targets {#targets}

Each target is measured on the composed film [choice, from the laws above]:

- The fighters' feet are moving for at least half of the fighting time. Translation [inference]: Dunn's elite amateur boxers stepped 64.0 ± 22.3 s and bounced 57.3 ± 27.3 s of a 180 s round, between about 72 s and 171 s of foot movement at one standard deviation below and above the means; kickboxers bounce less than boxers ("rarely do you see an athlete bouncing", `ONEFC-STANCE`) and stop moving their feet for each kick, so the film takes the lower half of that band, rounded to half the time, as its floor.
- At least a third of each fighter's footwork path runs across the line between the fighters rather than along it.
- The line between the fighters turns through at least one full revolution over the round.
- Each fighter has his back near the ropes at least once and for no more than a quarter of the round.
- Nobody steps outside the ropes, and no fighter crosses his feet.
