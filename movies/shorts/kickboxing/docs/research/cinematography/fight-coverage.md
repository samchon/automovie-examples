# Fight coverage

This study records how live fights are shot for television and how fiction films have shot them, so the screenplay's camera decisions follow a practice and the code can measure them. No original describing GLORY's or K-1's own camera plan, or a broadcast's convention for covering a knockdown count, was found; the practice below is boxing broadcast practice, carried over to a kickboxing ring. Each observation carries its kind as the [index](../index.md#observation-kinds) defines it; source ids are in the [index](../index.md#sources).

## Broadcast practice {#practice}

- [reported] HBO's ringside plan: "Two main cameras (set-up on tri-pods) are each responsible for covering both fighters in the ring at all times. One shoots both boxers head to toe in his frame, and the other follows the boxers from head to waist. There is one 90 Degree camera set up on the opposite side to get shots in the corners where the two main cameras cannot see"; "there are two handheld cameras being operated on the apron. The apron refers to the small two foot area of the ring that lies outside of the ropes" (`FERRO-HBO`).
- [reported] The same plan, copied for the film *Southpaw* with HBO's own operators: a camera "in the middle of the audience" that had "to see above the second rope", operators "ringside on the left and right … at the same level as the fighters"; "those cameras concentrate on the corners, but also follow the fight and zoom in according to the action"; takes ran "two to four rounds of boxing, with each round being three minutes long without a cut, covering it like a live televised fight" (`ASC-SOUTHPAW`).
- [reported] A practitioner's three-camera plan: two tripods, one on "head to toe framing" for context and one on "torso for strikes", and a ringside handheld that "MUST assume they are always live and move back and forth with the action"; with the two main cameras "roughly perpendicular to each other, so that if one camera is blocked by a boxer moving around, the other should capture it" (`DVINFO-BOXING`).
- [reported] Corner cameras: "an X-Mo was positioned in the far neutral corner" (`SVG-SHOWTIME-2014`); "a camera on a robotic head at one of the neutral corners of the ring" (`SVG-HBO-2017`); Sky's corner cameras "are at the height of the fighters" (`SVGEU-FROCH-GROVES`).
- [reported] Canvas-level cameras for knockdowns: ESPN's "bumper cam" units "buried in the mat" in two corners, "stabilized … in particular when the fighters hit the mat", give "a different angle shooting up at the fighters" (`SVG-WILDER-FURY`); NBC's "Mattcams" are "mounted flush with the top of the canvas on opposite sides of the ring", for "when a boxer is knocked out" (`SVG-NBC-2015`).
- [reported] A large broadcast used 38 cameras, including a jib over the ring and handheld and hard cameras "scattered throughout the arena" (`SVG-WILDER-FURY`). Operators work outside the ropes; in-ring cameras are unmanned (`FERRO-HBO`, `SVG-WILDER-FURY`).

## Fiction films {#fiction}

- [reported] *Raging Bull* kept the camera "inside the ropes", with POV shots, "constantly moving to keep up with the opponents" (`NFS-RAGING-BULL`, `ADORAMA-CREED`).
- [reported] *Creed*'s first fight is one Steadicam take of two ninety-second "movie rounds" and a minute's break, about four to four and a half minutes, ranging "between the sizes" from "that head-to-toe, big wide moment" to tight (`ASC-CREED`, `FM-CREED`). Its cinematographer rejected the *Raging Bull* approach as "stylized" in favour of something "more realistic", with rings that "looked more 'HBO than Raging Bull'" (`ASC-CREED`).
- [reported] Shot length: Bordwell describes Hollywood cutting moving "from an ASL range of 8-11 seconds before 1960 and towards a range of 4-6 seconds in recent years" (`CINEMETRICS-BORDWELL`), and "fast cutting, down to 2 or 3 seconds per shot on average" in today's intensified continuity (`BORDWELL-BLOG-2007`). The original 2002 essay was not accessible.

## Law {#law}

[choice, from the practice above]: the film is shot like a live broadcast, as *Southpaw* and *Creed* chose realism over stylization, with three cameras whose roles are the broadcast's:

- The hard camera: a tripod at ringside, raised so its view clears the ropes, framing both fighters head to toe with the referee; it is the reference view that orients the audience.
- The apron camera: a handheld on the apron, outside the ropes, that follows the action at the sizes from both men's full bodies to one man from the knees up, moving round the ring to stay side-on to the fighters' line.
- The corner camera: a fixed camera at fighter height in a neutral corner, the far-corner angle of broadcast practice.

The film cuts between them as a live director does, without replay or slow motion and without time compression, so every cut joins two views of continuous time. A cut changes camera, never time. Within a shot the camera moves only for a reason the action gives: to keep both fighters, to follow a man along the ropes, or to stay side-on as their line turns.

Broadcast practice keeps both fighters' feet, targets and heads readable: wide and medium framings hold both full bodies, and a closer framing is used only when one man's action is the point and the other is out of it. The two main viewpoints stand roughly perpendicular, so a fighter who blocks one view is seen from the other.

## The knockdown {#knockdown}

[reported] Broadcasts put dedicated cameras at the neutral corners and at canvas level for knockdowns (above); no original describes how a broadcast frames the count itself.

[choice]: the finishing kick and the fall are one shot, from the side where the kicking leg comes down, so neither man's body hides the leg, the struck place or the fall. The count is covered from the corner camera, which looks across the fallen man and the referee standing a step from him, as the [referee mechanics](../officiating/referee.md#knockdown) place him, at the standing fighter in the far neutral corner, the rule being kept in the background of the same frame. The ending returns to the hard camera's wide view.

## Measurable targets {#targets}

Each target is measured on the composed film [choice, from the practice above]:

- Mean shot length between 4 and 11 seconds; no shot shorter than 2 seconds; the finish shot is not counted. Translation [inference]: no source measures shot length in fight broadcasts. A live director cuts less often than an action editor, because each cut must keep a running fight readable, and the *Southpaw* operators covered three-minute rounds as live television; so the film's band runs from Bordwell's recent fiction range (4 to 6 s) up to his studio-era range (8 to 11 s), above his 2 to 3 s of intensified action cutting.
- In wide and medium shots both fighters' feet and heads are inside the frame for at least 80% of the time an exchange is live.
- During an exchange the line between the fighters lies within 45° of square to the camera's view, so feet, targets and heads share one screen axis.
- No camera stands inside the ropes; a moving camera's standpoint never jumps within a shot.
- From the start of the finishing kick to the fallen man at rest there is no cut, and the kicking leg, its contact and the struck place are never hidden by either man's body.
