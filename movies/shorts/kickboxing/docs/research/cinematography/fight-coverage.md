# docs/research/cinematography/fight-coverage.md: how a championship ring bout is shot, lit and heard

This study records how live broadcasts and fiction films shoot a ring bout, how a championship ring and its arena are lit, what the ring sounds like through broadcast microphones, how a fight crowd sits and sounds, and how the timekeeper's bell is placed and struck. It serves four records in the brief: cinematography (framing grammar, camera position and movement, cutting, lighting style and grade), sound (the ambience palette and the ring's own sounds), crowd (make-up, arrangement and reaction) and the arena set (lighting and layout around the ring). Shot-by-shot staging belongs to the screenplay (see [scope](#scope)).

## How to read this study {#reading}

Each observation carries its kind in brackets: **[commission]** for a value or ruling the commission fixes; **[measurement]** for a value with its unit that the original reports; **[observation]** for what the original's text states or its photograph shows; **[inference]** for this study's reasoning from cited facts; **[choice]** for a decision this production offers the brief where the sources leave room. Imperial values are converted to metric, with the original value kept in brackets. Every original was retrieved on 2026-11-13 and is listed, with its creator, URL, saved path, rights and the locators used, in the research index's [fight-coverage section](../index.md#originals-coverage). No footage could be decoded on this machine, so nothing here is read from moving pictures; any value footage alone would give is marked unavailable, inferred or chosen.

## Boxing coverage stands in for kickboxing {#translation}

Almost every source here covers boxing, with one UFC sound piece and one Muay Thai crowd study. Boxing is the right analogue because the ring is the same: a square, roped, raised platform with corners, neutral corners, a referee inside the ropes and a timekeeper at ringside (RULES [ring](../kickboxing/rules.md#ring), [officials](../kickboxing/rules.md#officials)) [inference]. Kicks change the coverage in four ways [inference]:

1. **The whole leg is the weapon.** Boxing's second main camera frames "head to waist" (HBO-OP para. 5); in kickboxing that size cuts off the kicking leg, the checking shin and the feet that set up every kick. The kickboxing baseline is the head-to-toe size, which boxing keeps as one of two main sizes.
2. **The frame needs room above the heads.** A high or Brazilian kick lifts the foot to head height or above, so a head-to-toe frame needs headroom for the raised leg as well as foot room under the planted foot.
3. **The fighting distance is longer.** Kicks reach farther than punches, so the two men stand farther apart at kicking range, and the referee stands farther off (referee study, [reading](../officiating/referee.md#reading)). A two-shot that holds both men head-to-toe must be wider than boxing's. The 16:9 frame suits this, as the commission notes ([frame](../../instructions/commission.md#frame)).
4. **The ring sounds include shin and instep.** Shin on thigh, shin on ribs, shin on a raised guard and instep on the head or neck join the glove contacts, and the kicking leg's support foot pivots and scrapes on the canvas.

## Broadcast coverage {#broadcast}

### The camera plan {#camera-plan}

- **HBO, five operators.** "Two main cameras (set-up on tri-pods) are each responsible for covering both fighters in the ring at all times. One shoots both boxers head to toe in his frame, and the other follows the boxers from head to waist." A "90 Degree" camera is "on the opposite side to get shots in the corners where the two main cameras cannot see", and two handheld cameras work on the apron (HBO-OP paras. 5–6) [observation].
- **HBO as re-created for Southpaw.** "HBO is specific about camera placement ... One camera is in the middle of the audience, and you get left and right of the ring. [They are] very specific as to height; you had to see above the second rope, meaning the fighters would be clear of all the ropes" (SOUTHPAW, para. beginning "To re-create an HBO-style experience") [observation]. The main positions carried an Angenieux HR 25–250 mm (T3.5) zoom [measurement].
- **DAZN, Milan.** "Cameras one and two were positioned along one side of the ring to film the long shot and the narrow shot of the match, respectively." A further camera sat at 90° with a long lens; an UltraEmotion camera sat "at 45°, between the main and 90° cameras"; two Jimmy-Jibs stood at ring corners, and "an elevated 'beauty camera' returned a wide shot of the whole ring" (DAZN-MIL paras. 7–9) [observation]. Main camera one carried a 17×6.2 zoom (para. 12) [measurement]. The ring was "isolated thanks to the presence of black sheets, creating a dark box" (para. 9) [observation].
- **Scale.** Sky covered Joshua–Klitschko with 16 ring cameras (SKY-AJ "Biggest ever fight for Sky Sports" para. 4); ESPN covered Wilder–Fury II with 38 (ESPN-WF para. 4); NBC used 24 for its biggest fights and 17–18 for lesser ones (NBC-LOOK para. 3); DAZN covered Benavidez–Yarde with 16 (DAZN-RIY "London to Riyadh") [measurement]. Yet "you'd be amazed at how much of the production comes from just three cameras and about as many microphones" (DAZN-AUD last para.) [observation].

The plan is broadly consistent: the main cameras sit together on one side of the ring at a fixed height, handhelds work the apron, and further cameras cover what the main side cannot see. HBO puts its 90-degree camera "on the opposite side" (HBO-OP para. 6), and DAZN placed a 45° camera between its main and 90° cameras (DAZN-MIL paras. 7–9) [observation; the summary is inference].

### Main camera height and distance {#main-cameras}

- Sky built its main camera platform "over two rows of seats at one end of the arena", because a ring needs "a different height and distance back" from a football pitch (SKY-AJ "Biggest ever fight" para. 5) [observation]. Its long-lens camera stands "60 feet [20m] away" (SKY-AJ "Camera positions through the ropes" para. 1) [measurement: 18.3–20 m].
- HBO's main cameras are set so the lens sees "above the second rope" (SOUTHPAW, as above) [observation]. MSG's high camera is "a huge pedestal camera ... in a steel deck above the audience"; the film's equivalent hung "40'–50' up" with a 24–290 mm zoom (SOUTHPAW, para. beginning "Another camera was above the ring") [measurement: 12.2–15.2 m].
- Sky has moved away from distance: "we don't really go for the long lens head-to-toe in-out coverage of many years ago ... There's a lot less filming from a long distance with ropes taking up half your screen" (SKY-AJ "Changing shooting styles" para. 1) [observation].

**Rope geometry** [inference]. GLORY's top rope is 1.50 m above the canvas, and the canvas is 0.90–1.22 m above the floor (RULES [ring](../kickboxing/rules.md#ring)). From a lens outside the ropes, the sightline to a man's feet clears the top rope only if the lens is at least 1.50 × (a + b) / b metres above the canvas. Here a is the lens's distance outside the rope line and b is the feet's distance inside it. With the feet at the centre of a 6.40 m ring (b = 3.2 m), that is 1.78 m for a lens 0.6 m outside the ropes, 4.3 m at 6 m out and 9.9 m at 18 m out. So a camera in the seats at fighter height always shoots through the ropes, and the ropes cross the men's legs. That is the "ropes taking up half your screen" Sky moved away from.

### Ringside handheld and apron cameras {#apron-cameras}

- HBO's two handhelds work "on the apron", "the small two foot area of the ring that lies outside of the ropes" (HBO-OP para. 6) [measurement: about 0.6 m]. Southpaw's HBO operators stood "on 2'x2' wooden camera platforms at the same level as the fighters". Those cameras "concentrate on the corners, but also follow the fight and zoom in according to the action", and "as the fighters go into their corners, each [operator] takes a fighter" (SOUTHPAW, paras. beginning "Palladino and Cypher were positioned" and "Todd and Rick cover everything") [observation]. They used Fujinon Cabrio 19–90 mm (T2.9) zooms and pulled their own focus [measurement].
- The apron camera is close and exposed. Fighters pressing on the ropes once put "500 LBS" against them, and the operator jumped off (HBO-OP para. 8) [observation]. An operator anticipates "the direction the boxer will go", and gloves have hit the camera (SOUTHPAW, para. beginning "Palladino adds") [observation].
- When fighters are across the ring or infighting with a back to the judges, "television usually switches to a camera on the ring apron for a better view" (JUDGE "The Angle" para. 2) [observation].
- DAZN's ringside handhelds give "immediate shallow depth of field" because "you've got the boxers right in front of you" (DAZN-RIY para. beginning "Cinematic cameras") [observation].

### Low, canvas-level and overhead cameras {#low-cameras}

- Sky's cameras five and six are "low down shooting up through the ropes, to get artistic low angles, including of the corner men" (SKY-AJ "Camera positions through the ropes" para. 2) [observation].
- NBC mounted two robotic super-slow-motion Mattcams "flush with the top of the canvas on opposite sides of the ring", for a view "when a boxer is knocked out" (NBC-LOOK paras. 11–12) [observation]. ESPN buried "bumper cam" lipstick cameras with wide-angle lenses in two ring corners to catch fighters "coming down perfectly when they hit the mat" (ESPN-WF paras. 13–14) [observation].
- Overheads: HBO's normal overhead was "a standard fixed robotic overhead" (HBO-AER para. 5); NBC ran an 80 ft (24.4 m) JitaCam track with a 30 ft (9.1 m) boom (NBC-LOOK para. 13); Sky added a ringside jib for "nice close overhead shots of the boxers" (SKY-AJ "Aerial display" para. 5) and a Batcam Wire under the O2's lighting truss (SKY-BAT paras. 4–5) [observation]. Sky's aim for the wire camera: "If we can capture the boxer looking up at the ceiling from the canvas, that is the money shot" (SKY-BAT pull quote) [observation].

An overhead lays the two men on the canvas plane. It shows their spacing but not their heads, targets and feet on one screen axis, so it cannot carry an exchange under the commission's [camera](../../instructions/commission.md#camera) requirement [inference]. Canvas-level and wire cameras that hunt the downed man's face are knockout-replay tools [inference].

### Keeping both fighters and their feet in frame {#feet-in-frame}

- HBO's head-to-toe main camera covers "both fighters in the ring at all times" (HBO-OP para. 5) [observation].
- Sky's front jib keeps head-to-toe coverage close: "I'll usually go to the jib head-to-toe rather than come all the way out to a long lens camera 60 feet [20m] away because it takes you away from it" (SKY-AJ "Camera positions through the ropes" para. 1) [observation].
- Sky comes out to the head-to-toe size at resets: "We tend to come out head-to-toe when the referee says 'break' and everyone's had to move back, not when a boxer is in the middle of a really big exchange" (SKY-AJ last para.) [observation].
- Creed held both men sharp: "In the ring I tried to keep a stop of T5.6 so we could have both fighters in focus" (CREED, para. beginning "Alberti shot Creed") [measurement].

The commission's "feet, targets and heads readable together on the same screen axis" is the broadcast head-to-toe two-shot taken from the side, with the men's line of fight across the frame [inference]. The referee seeks the same side view (referee study), so the main camera and the referee often share an axis, which is why the referee blocks it (next section) [inference].

### The referee in the shot {#referee-occlusion}

- "If you only use distant cameras and a handheld ringside camera, every time the referee gets in shot you have to cut, whereas when you've got a jib, if the ref gets in the way, they can just move around past him, so it's much less distracting than having to keep cutting" (SKY-AJ "Changing shooting styles" para. 1) [observation].
- A judge's view is often blocked by the referee, while "television ... can easily switch angles" (JUDGE "The Angle" paras. 3–4) [observation].
- A referee camera is of limited use: "Boxing referees often walk backwards, so they are looking at the boxing, but their bodies are facing away" (SKY-AJ "Changing technology" para. 3) [observation].

A camera that moves sideways around the referee, rather than a cut, is the broadcast answer to occlusion. It suits a production whose kick and fall must play without a cut [inference].

### Cutting between angles {#cutting}

- "The thing that's so distracting if you watch coverage of boxing the first time people do it [is that] they just keep cutting the cameras because they're moving so fast. What we're trying to do is slow it all down and go to cameras at the right time" (SKY-AJ last para.) [observation]. Over-cutting marks an inexperienced director; cutting at resets marks an experienced one [inference].
- "The fight dictates the coverage." Lighter men "move around the ring very fast, so we'll cut the cameras a lot less", while heavier men "stay in pockets for a bit longer, so perhaps we cut round it a little bit more" (SKY-AJ "Camera positions through the ropes" para. 1) [observation].
- Southpaw: "It doesn't matter if you see the other camera because that adds to the accuracy; [that is what] you see in a live event" (SOUTHPAW, para. beginning "Todd and Rick") [observation].
- Between rounds, broadcasters show replays, often in super slow motion (DAZN-MIL last para.; SKY-AJ "Biggest ever fight" para. 6) [observation]. The commission excludes replay ([sound](../../instructions/commission.md#sound)), so this film has no such insert [commission].

### Screen direction and the 180-degree line {#screen-direction}

No retrieved source states a line rule for ring coverage [observation]. The camera plans imply a reference side: the main cameras sit together "along one side of the ring" (DAZN-MIL para. 7), and the apron handhelds take the corners (HBO-OP; SOUTHPAW) [observation]. HBO's 90-degree camera works from "the opposite side" for the corners the main cameras cannot see (HBO-OP para. 6) [observation]. Most coverage therefore comes from the main side, which is the reference, with reverse angles from the opposite side for what it cannot see [inference]. Fighters circle, so their line keeps turning and they swap screen sides within one uncut shot. Viewers keep track by corner colour and trunks, not by fixed screen position [inference]. Wilder–Fury's corner cameras sat in "two corners" (ESPN-WF para. 13) and a robotic camera sat in "the far neutral corner" (ESPN-SND para. 10), so broadcasts do use reverse angles [observation].

### Shot length {#shot-length}

No retrieved source measures shot length or cut rate in boxing or kickboxing broadcasts. A search for a measured study found none, and the footage could not be decoded here [unavailable]. What the sources give instead:

- Cutting slows for faster, lighter men and quickens for slower, heavier ones (SKY-AJ, as above) [observation].
- At the referee's "break" Sky comes out to the head-to-toe size, "not when a boxer is in the middle of a really big exchange" (SKY-AJ last para.) [observation]. For heavier men who "stay in pockets", "perhaps we cut round it a little bit more" (SKY-AJ "Camera positions through the ropes" para. 1) [observation]. That a sober film keeps its cuts to such resets is this production's choice [choice].
- Fiction long takes: Creed's first fight is one Steadicam shot covering two 90 s "movie rounds" and the 60 s break between them. Each of its 12 takes ran 4 min (CREED, para. beginning "Alberti describes") [measurement: one shot of about 240 s]. Southpaw's HBO operators rolled "two to four rounds of boxing, with each round being three minutes long without a cut, covering it like a live televised fight" (SOUTHPAW, para. beginning "The filmmakers turned to HBO") [measurement]. Those are camera takes, not shots in the edit.

## Fiction films in the ring {#fiction}

### Creed: one take that searches {#creed}

- Coogler and Alberti chose not to follow Raging Bull: "Ryan wanted the boxing to be more realistic and less stylized". They took from A Prophet its "mix of Steadicam, handheld and static shots, and how each choice of technique was motivated by the story" (CREED, para. beginning "During prep") [observation].
- "Ryan's filmmaking style calls for a very inquisitive and curious camera that moves and searches. It's not a quiet and composed camera" (CREED, next para.) [observation].
- The one-shot fight: two 90 s rounds and the 60 s break in a single Steadicam shot, 12 takes of 4 min, with operator Benjamin Semanoff memorising "the complex choreography" (CREED, para. beginning "Alberti describes") [measurement]. The reason given: "a boxer is completely alone when he enters the ring" (next para.) [observation].
- What the moving camera looks for: "There is always a fine line between being there with the camera at the right moment — seeing the movement and the glove connecting with the face — and preserving the human drama ... I always wanted to come back and have the drama of the face looking into the camera" (CREED, para. beginning "Boxing is a particularly improvisational sport") [observation].
- The final fight used more cameras: a Movi passed through the ropes, a Movi "circling the fighters in and out of the ring as they were against the ropes", a Technocrane, a Steadicam and two handhelds (CREED, para. beginning "The final fight") [observation].
- Lenses: Cooke S4/i primes and Angenieux Optimo short zooms, at T5.6 in the ring (CREED, para. beginning "Alberti shot Creed") [measurement].

### Southpaw: broadcast grammar, then subjectivity {#southpaw}

- The aim for the fights was "Reality ... As close to reality as you could possibly get in a film". The first fight "is very observational, with an objective viewpoint", and later fights grow subjective (SOUTHPAW, paras. beginning "As Gyllenhaal was training" and "Second was to tell") [observation].
- The fighters went "all the rounds of each fight without stopping, and we were rolling the whole time". There were "monitors for every camera, the same way you would in TV coverage" (SOUTHPAW, paras. beginning "Fuqua's ambitious plan" and "Each fight was highly choreographed") [observation].
- Camera set: six Alexa XTs to an HBO plan; the two HBO operators at ringside; a high camera 12.2–15.2 m (40–50 ft) up; an Alexa on a Giraffe crane with a remote head "12' off the ground in one of the corners" [measurement: 3.7 m], used "for alternate-angle knockout shots" (SOUTHPAW, paras. beginning "Another camera" and "An Alexa was mounted") [observation].
- Stylising devices: a Movi in the ring that can "put the camera almost on the ground and lift it up to somebody's face"; a Lensbaby "for Billy's disoriented point of view"; a camera mounted on the actor; "Alexa and Phantom cameras ... for slow-motion knockout sequences" and "the Phantom for the final knockout" (SOUTHPAW, paras. beginning "The second fight", "An Alexa was mounted" and "By this point") [observation].

### Raging Bull: deliberate stylisation {#raging-bull}

- "It's not at all a realistic movie and the fights particularly are ... very deliberately not realistic". They "were like dances ... very, very elaborately choreographed with camera moves and ... steps for the actors all worked out". Each fight had its own style: "one was done all with long lenses, one was done ... tried to make long ... long takes as long as we could" (CHAPMAN-46 transcript) [observation].
- Frame rate rules: "the actual boxing would all be at 24 frames". The famous slow shots come when the fighter is not boxing, as when "you go to 48 frames while Jake walks away in the neutral corner and he's breathing, and he comes back to 24 frames when he's going to fight again, but it's all in one shot". In the corner they went to "96 or 120 frames". "When they were boxing we made sure they were always 24" (CHAPMAN-47 transcript) [measurement].
- "In the ring it's extremely elaborate and full of all sorts of camera tricks and moves", while outside it "the camera just sits there and watches" (CHAPMAN-48 transcript, para. 1) [observation].
- The sound was stylised too: a different sound for "each punch in the various fight sequences" and for "every camera click and flashbulb", with background sound dropping "to nothing" before a blow, and a "rumbling atmosphere" replacing the crowd until "the audience roar returns" (RB-SND "Landing the punches" and "Use of silence") [observation].

### When fiction cuts {#fiction-cutting}

Creed holds two rounds and the break between them in one shot (CREED) [observation]. Southpaw shot every round continuously from several cameras and cut in the edit among broadcast-style angles, POV and slow-motion knockouts (SOUTHPAW) [observation]. Raging Bull cut to a designed rhythm per fight (CHAPMAN-46; RB-SND) [observation]. A long take in these films tends to cover a whole round or a whole sustained action, and the cut comes at a reset, a corner or a change of viewpoint [inference]. This production's uncut kick and fall and its real-time count follow the Creed model ([finish](../../instructions/commission.md#fall), [count](../../instructions/commission.md#count)) [commission].

## Sober, close coverage against highlight style {#sober-coverage}

### Lenses and focus {#lenses}

- Close coverage uses short-to-normal focal lengths from close by, not long lenses from far off. Sky prefers the jib "rather than ... a long lens camera 60 feet [20m] away because it takes you away from it" (SKY-AJ) [observation]. Raging Bull used "all long lenses" for one fight as a deliberate style (CHAPMAN-46) [observation].
- Reported lenses: Creed Cooke S4/i primes and Optimo short zooms at T5.6 (CREED); Southpaw ringside 19–90 mm, main 25–250 mm, overhead 24–290 mm, in-ring Cooke MiniS4 on a Movi (SOUTHPAW); DAZN main 17×6.2 broadcast zoom (DAZN-MIL) [measurement]. These are ranges for different sensor formats. The brief converts any focal length it adopts into the renderer's field of view using the renderer's own camera model [inference].
- Depth of field: Creed stopped down so both fighters stayed sharp (CREED); DAZN welcomes the shallow focus of its close handhelds (DAZN-RIY) [observation]. A shallow focus that drops one fighter breaks the commission's rule that both men are readable together [inference].

### Framing {#framing}

- Broadcast sizes are head-to-toe and head-to-waist on the main cameras, close and corner shots on the apron (HBO-OP) [observation]. Kickboxing keeps head-to-toe as the baseline ([translation](#translation)) [inference].
- Creed returns to "the face looking into the camera" between the contacts (CREED) [observation]. In a two-shot, a face is readable in a head-to-toe frame only from close in, which is a reason to bring the camera into or onto the ring rather than zoom from the seats [inference].

### Camera motion {#motion}

- Sober motion reacts to the fighters. Sky's jib gives "a gentle, fluid move ... you don't have to keep cutting" and allows "lateral movement so that you feel like you're inside the ring" (SKY-AJ "Camera positions through the ropes" para. 5; "Changing shooting styles" para. 1) [observation]. Creed's camera "moves and searches" (CREED) [observation]. Southpaw's HBO operators "anticipate the direction the boxer will go" (SOUTHPAW) [observation].
- Highlight motion is the camera's own display: wire cameras flown to "trick the viewer" (DAZN-RIY "Trick shots" para. 3), 360-degree replay rigs (ESPN-WF para. 15; NBC-LOOK para. 14) and "an active, chaotic camera" for a subjective state (SOUTHPAW, para. beginning "By the end of the second fight") [observation].

### No slow motion {#no-slow-motion}

- Broadcasters own many slow-motion cameras: seven high-speed units at Wilder–Fury II (ESPN-WF para. 16); three X-Mo systems able to run "above 2,000 fps" (NBC-LOOK para. 10); a 500 fps UltraEmotion in Milan (DAZN-MIL para. 6) [measurement]. They use them for replays between rounds (DAZN-MIL last para.) [observation].
- Even Sky's director is wary of slow motion: "if you see a punch in hi-mo, whichever way you go, it looks quite brutal" (SKY-AJ "Biggest ever fight" para. 6) [observation].
- Raging Bull, the most stylised of these films, still kept the boxing itself at 24 fps (CHAPMAN-47) [measurement].
- The commission rules out replay and time compression ([sound](../../instructions/commission.md#sound)), and its tone rules out relishing the violence ([tone](../../instructions/commission.md#tone)). Every frame of this film runs at real speed [choice, following the commission].

| Colour | A palette per fight (SOUTHPAW) | One realistic grade for the round (CREED) [choice] |
| Sound | Designed punches, silence before a blow, animal or rumble layers (RB-SND; TONE 27:16) | The ring's own sounds, each contact distinct, crowd under them (commission [sound](../../instructions/commission.md#sound)) [choice] |

## Arena and ring lighting {#lighting}

### Even, bright ring light {#ring-light}

- Creed's rings looked "more 'HBO than Raging Bull'", lit "from a truss circling the perimeter with 120 Par cans narrow-focused across the ring, medium-focused in the middle, and wide-focused up close, which gave a very even lighting all across the ring" (CREED, para. beginning "Alberti adhered") [measurement: 120 fixtures].
- Southpaw hung "156 Par cans on the truss immediately surrounding the ring" plus moving lights, with a soft box over the centre "to sculpt the light around our boxers" (SOUTHPAW, paras. beginning "Fiore describes" and "To light the actors") [measurement].
- Gatto's broadcast rig hung "Seventy-two 575Wt ETC Source Four Pars and 24 High End Systems Studio Colors" for ring and audience. He diffuses "every ring light" with Gam 65 or Rosco 104 "to soften it", meters "all areas of the ring to check for shadows", and backs the dimmers off to 80% (GATTO paras. 5 and 7) [measurement]. "You never want to have harsh shadow" (para. 7) [observation].
- Level: anchor lights "no more than 175fc, which is similar to the level in the ring" (GATTO para. 6) [measurement: about 1,880 lx]. Mooney's LED ring light runs "at 5800K and running about 140 ft candles" (BYB "Lighting the ring" para. 2) [measurement: about 1,510 lx at 5,800 K]. A championship ring is lit to about 1,500–1,900 lx [measurement range, two sources].
- Avoiding spill and shadow: "You need to avoid light spillage outside of the ring but you also need to avoid casting shadows in the ring" (BYB "Lighting the ring" para. 1) [observation]. Mooney runs moving lights as spots and ballyhoos only between rounds; "during the action, being careful not to flash light through the ring", they go to wall patterns while washes "keep the audience lit for the camera shot and depth of view" (BYB "Wall and audience lighting") [observation].
- During the bout, Britain's Boxing Board of Control is "very strict on us not having lights up once boxing is going on in the ring, because they don't obviously want anything to distract the boxer" (SKY-AJ "A Box Office occasion" para. 3) [observation].

### Rig height and the arena roof {#rig-height}

- Mooney's ring PARs carry R104 diffusion "as long as the truss is hung at his specified 35'" (ROSCO entry) [measurement: 10.7 m]. DAZN's Riyadh centre-hung truss with screens and lights "normally trims to about 12 metres" (DAZN-RIY "Trick shots" para. 2) [measurement]. Wembley's canopy over the ring was "30-feet [10m]" (SKY-AJ "Aerial display" para. 4) [measurement]. The ring rig hangs about 10–12 m above the floor [measurement range, three sources].
- Order of build: "Cameras ringside can't go in until the ring is in; the ring can't go in until the lighting truss has been lifted" (SKY-AJ "Biggest ever fight" para. 8) [observation].

### A dark audience {#audience-light}

- "You never want to have same amount of light in ring as on the audience ... We put a slightly amber tone light on the audience at half the intensity level, then we fade it lightly as you go back ... the key is not to draw attention away from ring" (GATTO para. 8) [observation]. Half the level is one stop below the ring [inference].
- Mooney washes the audience through a coloured E-Colour 105 gel "because it gives the camera separation between audience and ring" (ROSCO entry) [observation].
- Gatto's audience PARs were "many in color, to separate the crowd from the main focus of the ring" (GATTO para. 5) [observation].
- In Milan, black drapes isolated the ring as "a dark box" (DAZN-MIL para. 9) [observation].

The pattern is a bright, even, white ring; ringside rows lit about one stop lower in a warm tint; and the light falling away into dark tiers [inference]. The commission's "seated crowd in the dark beyond the ring" ([venue](../../instructions/commission.md#venue)) sits at the dark end of that real practice [inference]. As a choice, ringside rows sit about 1 stop under the ring, the lower tier 3–4 stops under it, and upper tiers near black, with faces readable only in the first rows [choice].

### Colour and grade {#colour-grade}

- Creed's palette was "grounded in realism"; the grade built on the dailies LUT with touch-ups to skin; "red is the color that is going to [be picked] up first" in digital (CREED, paras. beginning "Alberti describes", "Thanks to the dailies LUT") [observation].
- Southpaw gave each fight its own palette: "Madison Square Garden blue, with lots of blue moving lights — a cool, observational palette", then greens, then warmer reds and golds (SOUTHPAW, para. beginning "The university location") [observation].
- Ring light colour: 5,800 K white from Mooney's LEDs (BYB) [measurement]; a slightly amber audience (GATTO) [observation].

As a choice, the film holds one neutral grade for the whole round: near-neutral white on the ring, a slight amber in the ringside rows, no per-phase colour shifts and no coloured beams in the round [choice].

## Ring sound in broadcast {#ring-sound}

### Microphones and where they hang {#microphones}

- **Over the ring.** ESPN's "Tiffany" array: "six Sennheiser 8060 mini-shotgun microphones, four facing outward to pick up ringside-crowd noise and two facing directly downward". The downward pair "pick up most of the footwork and punches — '99.5% of that'". It normally hangs "9.5 ft. above the ring" and was raised "a full foot higher" for two very tall fighters (ESPN-SND para. 8) [measurement: 2.9–3.2 m above the canvas]. NBC strung "a pair of Audio-Technica AT815B gradient-condenser microphones ... nearly 8 ft. above the center of the ring" (NBC-MUTE para. 4) [measurement: about 2.4 m]. HBO hung 416 shotguns "over the ring and from neutral corners" (HBO-FIB para. 5) [observation]. DAZN hung a condenser on its cable camera "to catch ring effects" (DAZN-AUD para. 4) [observation].
- **Corners.** ESPN taped lavalier microphones to the bottom rope, "angled at 90 degrees and pointed toward and about 2 ft. away from each fighter's corner" (ESPN-SND para. 6) [measurement: about 0.6 m]. NBC used Sennheiser 416 short shotguns "in each corner"; HBO used 816 shotguns on fishpoles "at the red and blue corners" (NBC-MUTE para. 4; HBO-FIB para. 5) [observation].
- **People.** Referees and trainers wear lavaliers at NBC, ESPN, DAZN Milan and UFC (NBC-MUTE para. 4; ESPN-SND para. 5; DAZN-MIL para. 14; UFC-SND para. 3) [observation]. In Britain, Sky could not get the referee miked: "That'd be perfect for us because you'd hear all those punches close up" (SKY-AJ "Changing technology" para. 4) [observation].
- **The canvas.** UFC uses "a kick-drum type of microphone ... to amplify the thumps of bodies hitting canvas floor", and PZM boundary microphones "attached face down on the mat" (UFC-SND paras. 3 and 7) [observation]. DAZN considered "placing a microphone under the mat to pick up foot movement" (DAZN-AUD para. 9) [observation].
- **Cameras and crowd.** Shotguns ride on handheld cameras. Sky's sound operator follows the director's camera: "if I'm on camera three handheld, he'll be on that camera's microphone ... If I go to the Steadicam in the crowd, they'll be on that Steadicam so they get all the cheering effects" (SKY-AJ "Changing shooting styles" para. 2). Further 416s and a stereo microphone face the audience (NBC-MUTE para. 4; UFC-SND para. 5) [observation]. Close to 30 microphones served DAZN's largest show (DAZN-AUD para. 7) [measurement].

### What the ring microphones pick up {#what-is-heard}

- Footwork and punches, mostly from the overhead pair (ESPN-SND para. 8) [observation].
- Bodies hitting the canvas, through a low-frequency microphone (UFC-SND para. 3) [observation].
- Corner coaching: "wired mics on the corners to pick up coaches' calling out directions during the matches" (DAZN-AUD para. 4) [observation].
- The bell: the commentators' headset microphones "pick up transients, such as the bell signaling the start and end of rounds" (NBC-MUTE para. 7) [observation].
- The referee, through his lavalier and through the house PA as ambient sound (NBC-MUTE para. 7) [observation].
- Distance matters: punches near the ropes are easy to pick up, while in the centre "it's harder to get the sound ... You have to constantly watch and adjust for that" (UFC-SND para. 6) [observation].
- Film sound editors add what microphones miss. They layer the feet "almost [like a] subharmonic pass", the swings' whooshes, and "the rope ... with the metal and then the creek" (TONE, Olivia Zhang 18:01) [observation]. A boxing ring "is like a plywood thing", and feet "can also be a little bit distracting" (TONE, René Coronado 17:32) [observation].
- Breath: a host who boxed found that in the ring "the crowd goes away ... except for your own breath" (TONE, René Coronado 19:32) [observation]. Mixers leave "that pocket for the breath or the reaction relative to the transient of the hit", sometimes staggering them by "two frames" (TONE, Aaron Glasscock 48:40) [observation].

### Mixing crowd against ring {#mix}

- "Boxing ... takes its audio in from everywhere all the time" (NBC-MUTE para. 2) [observation]. The crowd is "a constant presence in the broadcast mix" (UFC-SND para. 5) [observation].
- "The trick will be balancing the sound from the ring without losing any of the excitement you hear in the audience. It's a very noisy place" (NBC-MUTE last para.) [observation].
- Crowd in a film mix: "With a really big crowds, you have to be very careful because it eventually turns into like a 10k sandblaster. So again, this idea of relativity and being able to come back down so you can go back up" (TONE, Aaron Glasscock 44:07) [observation]. One crowd designer kept the crowd away from the centre channel so it did not compete with the fight, and chose crowd frequencies to sit around the hits (TONE, Olivia Zhang 38:53) [observation].
- No retrieved source gives the level, in decibels, of ring sounds or crowd in a broadcast mix [unavailable].

### No commentary {#no-commentary}

Every broadcast covered here has commentators, and the commentary is mixed over the ring and crowd (NBC-MUTE "A Busy Mix"; JUDGE para. on announcers) [observation]. With commentary removed, what remains is the bed broadcasters already build: overhead ring effects, corner and referee microphones, and the crowd [inference]. The commission rules out commentary, music score and replay voices ([sound](../../instructions/commission.md#sound)). Between-round promoter music (SKY-AJ "Changing shooting styles" para. 3) falls outside a single round [observation]. During the round no PA music or announcement plays [inference from the BBBofC's no-distraction rule, SKY-AJ "A Box Office occasion" para. 3].

### Voices at ringside {#corner-voices}

- Corners shout: "Cornermen are rarely silent ... 'There you go!' or 'He didn't like that!'" (JUDGE para. on cornermen) [observation]. An apron operator can "hear the corner people (trainers, friends, family) yelling" (HBO-OP para. 6) [observation].
- Crowds swear, and US broadcasts run a delay to mute it (NBC-MUTE paras. 1–3). A "persistent and annoyingly loud whistler" reached the mix (NBC-MUTE para. 7) [observation].
- GLORY seats the cornermen and prohibits "excessive coaching" during the match (RULES [corner teams](../kickboxing/rules.md#corner-teams)) [observation].

The commission keeps the referee's words as the only speech, and the crowd wordless ([language](../../instructions/commission.md#language), [venue](../../instructions/commission.md#venue)). Real corners and crowds shout words, so this is a departure. The film's corners make no words during the round, and the crowd's shouts are vowel calls, whistles and claps without words [choice].

## The fight crowd {#crowd}

### Arrangement and make-up {#crowd-arrangement}

- At Wembley, floor seating went round the ring, with "ringside commentary positions and tables" and the main camera platform built "over two rows of seats at one end" (SKY-AJ "Biggest ever fight" paras. 5 and 8) [observation]. Seats near the corner jib "have to be sold as restricted view" (SKY-AJ "Camera positions through the ropes" para. 3) [observation].
- ESPN calls the floor seats "the most rambunctious", with "high levels of enthusiasm" (ESPN-SND para. 10) [observation].
- Broadcast positions sit high: "in the balcony to give the overview effect", "high up in the grandstand" (GATTO paras. 6 and 10) [observation].
- Officials sit at ringside: the timekeeper at the officials' table beside the supervisor (BIBA p. 1), the judges round the ring (MYERS "Procedure") [observation]. In Muay Thai events, crowds ranged "from 500 to 3000, with proximity between judges and crowd varying from two to several meters" (MYERS "Procedure") [measurement].
- A championship arena: Riyadh's ANB Arena holds "around 6,000" (DAZN-RIY "Engaging audiences" para. 4) [measurement]. Southpaw shot "massive audience plates" of a "center block" at Madison Square Garden to rebuild the arena in effects (SOUTHPAW, para. beginning "The plate shoot") [observation].
- Make-up: no retrieved source describes a fight crowd's age, sex or dress [unavailable]. As a choice, the brief writes an adult mixed crowd in ordinary evening clothes, with the first rows in jackets, and no flash photography during the round [choice].

The layout is a ring on the arena floor, ringside tables for officials, press and commentary, rows of floor seats round all four sides, then raked tiers rising into the dark, with the lighting truss and centre-hung screens above the ring [inference from the above].

### How a fight crowd sounds {#crowd-sound}

- The crowd is "vocally supporting" a favourite, and its noise follows the action. One hypothesis is that judges hear "greater crowd noise equating to more effective strikes" (MYERS "Discussion: why an impact of crowd noise" para. 1; "Limitations of the study") [observation]. The study found a live crowd's noise moved judges by about half a point a bout (MYERS "Discussion: summary of findings") [measurement]. Its own limitations note that its design "reduced the possibility of determining whether crowd noise is used as a cue", so it does not show how a live crowd answers each blow (MYERS "Limitations of the study") [observation]. No retrieved source measures that [unavailable].
- Crowd as call and response: "you're playing off the crowd because you're signaling that call and response ... that's the real time facet that the spectator feeds off of that's the dashboard, the hits as they progress" (TONE, Aaron Glasscock 24:08) [observation]. A crowd designer builds "little apexes and events" on the reactions (TONE, Aaron Glasscock 40:40) [observation].
- Sound editors use hockey crowds for clean reactions because the NHL bars PA music during play: "anyone takes a really nice check, you get ... these big reacts" (TONE, René Coronado 44:57) [observation].
- Crowd size changes the sound: Creed II's fights ranged from clubs of "a couple 100 people" to "this massive Russian arena" (TONE, René Coronado 41:50) [observation].
- Constant crowd tires the ear; a mix must "come back down so you can go back up" (TONE, Aaron Glasscock 44:07) [observation].

As a choice built on the film crowd designers' call-and-response practice above (TONE), not on a live measurement: the crowd stays present as a low bed, swells on each landed exchange, peaks on a big kick and falls back when the men reset. It does not track every feint [choice].

### The knockout reaction {#knockout-reaction}

- No retrieved source measures or describes, from a live venue, the crowd's sound at a knockout [unavailable].
- Fiction uses silence. Raging Bull drops background sound "to nothing" before a blow, and replaces the crowd with a rumble before "the audience roar returns" (RB-SND "Use of silence") [observation]. Sound designers describe the moment where "everything is just wiped out and the hit just goes in silence" (TONE, Aaron Glasscock 27:16) [observation].
- This is a cinematic device, not something a live crowd does [inference]. A sober film keeps the crowd real [choice].

As a choice: at the landing, a sharp collective roar starts within a fraction of a second of the contact; it holds through the fall; it settles to a murmur with scattered calls under the referee's count, so each spoken number stays clear; it rises again at the knockout signal; and it holds high through the bell and celebration while staying under the ring's sounds. The crowd does not count along and is never silent [choice].

### How dark the crowd is in picture {#crowd-in-picture}

From the lighting practice above, the crowd reads in picture as a dim, warm field of heads and shoulders at ringside, falling into silhouette and then darkness behind (GATTO; ROSCO; DAZN-MIL) [inference]. Faces read only in the first rows, and only from a camera looking past the fighters toward them [inference]. The commission's crowd "in the dark" fits this [inference].

## The timekeeper's bell {#bell}

### Where it is {#bell-position}

- "A gong not less than twelve inches in diameter shall be fastened securely to one of the ring supports or other suitable place at the outside center of the ring on a level with the ring platform" (HAR § 16-74-296) [measurement: diameter at least 305 mm].
- BIBA puts the timekeeper "next to [the supervisor] at the Officials Table Ringside" (BIBA p. 1). England Boxing seats the timekeeper "at ringside to the far right of the blue corner" (EB-TK slide 2) [observation]. GLORY's text is silent on the position; RULES records Ontario's "side with no judge, near the doctor" (RULES [officials](../kickboxing/rules.md#officials)) [observation].

The bell is a gong at the officials' table or fixed to the ring's side at canvas level, at the centre of one side, beside the timekeeper [inference]. In picture it sits at the foot of the ring, below the ropes [inference].

### How it is struck {#bell-strike}

- The timekeeper "shall use a metal hammer to indicate the beginning and end of rounds" (HAR) [observation]. The TITLE professional gong is "Heavy duty 10” gong ... mounted to hardwood" with an "11” hammer" on a chain (TITLE product description) [measurement: 254 mm gong, 279 mm hammer].
- The timekeeper "must start and end each round by striking the bell" (ARMY-TK item b; EB-TK slide 3) [observation].
- Warnings use a separate sound. The ten-second warning is a gavel struck twice with a raised left arm (BIBA p. 2) or three times (ARMY-TK item c) [measurement: 2–3 strikes]. TITLE's gong has a "hard plastic strike plate" for those warnings (TITLE) [observation]. RULES finds that no ten-second warning falls inside this film ([bells](../kickboxing/rules.md#bells)) [observation].
- Under boxing rules a gavel marks the seconds of a knockdown count (EB-TK slide 6; ARMY-TK item e). GLORY has no knockdown timekeeper, and the film has none (RULES [anchors](../kickboxing/rules.md#anchors)) [observation]. No gavel sounds during the count [inference].
- The number of strikes at a bout's end is not stated in any retrieved source [unavailable]. Timing belongs to RULES ([match-ending bell](../kickboxing/rules.md#match-bell)): 170.1 s, 2.5 s after the knockout signal.

### How it sounds {#bell-sound}

- No retrieved source measures the bell's pitch, level or decay [unavailable].
- The gong must be loud enough "that the contestants and referee can hear the sound of the bell" (HAR) [observation]. Broadcast headsets pick it up as a transient (NBC-MUTE para. 7) [observation]. It is a sharp metallic strike that carries over crowd noise [inference].

As a choice: the opening bell is a single strike; the match-ending bell is a run of three or four quick strikes; each strike has a hard metal attack and a ringing decay of about 2–3 s, heard from the timekeeper's side of the ring [choice].

## Scope {#scope}

Against the [research statements](../../../../../../.agents/skills/contracts/story/shared/statements/principles/research.md#depicted-practice):

- **1. Technique laws** (poses, phases, reach, support, travel): *the subject has none.* A camera, lighting or sound style has no body technique. The fighters' techniques belong to the screenplay's later study.
- **2. Legal and illegal actions:** *the subject has none of its own.* The bout's rules are in RULES. The one conduct rule for coverage found here, no extra lights raised during the boxing, is recorded under [ring light](#ring-light).
- **3. Outcome table:** *the subject has none.* A camera style has no outcomes. The bout's outcome table is in RULES ([outcomes](../kickboxing/rules.md#outcomes)).
- **4. How actions chain:** covered for coverage as [cutting](#cutting) at resets and [long takes](#fiction-cutting). *The chain of shots across the round belongs to the screenplay's later study.*
- **5. Spatial strategy and tempo across the duration:** the camera's tempo is covered qualitatively under [cutting](#cutting). The fighters' use of the ring and the round's tempo, at the level of style, are in the [styles study](../kickboxing/styles.md#pressure-ring-and-tempo). *Their measured rates and the path of each exchange belong to the screenplay's later study.*
- **6. Conduct of everyone present:** covered for camera operators, the crowd, corners as heard and the timekeeper. The referee's conduct is in the [referee study](../officiating/referee.md). The corner teams' rules are in RULES.
- **7. Markers of the depicted level:** covered for coverage: over-cutting marks an inexperienced director ([cutting](#cutting)). The fighters' markers are in the [styles study](../kickboxing/styles.md#level-markers).
- **8. How the genre is shot:** covered ([broadcast](#broadcast), [fiction](#fiction), [sober coverage](#sober-coverage)).
- **9–10. Measurable targets as ranges:** covered where a source gives them ([targets](#targets)). Shot-change cadence and sound levels are unavailable. *Distance by phase, action rate and idle time per presence belong to the screenplay's later study.*
- **11. An invented action's analogue:** the bout is fictional and its coverage borrows boxing's. The translation is in [translation](#translation).
- **12–13. No adjective as a value; every value sourced:** values carry sources. Production choices are marked [choice].
- **14–15. Package exports:** no installed package exports a camera, lighting, crowd or sound style. The engine stages shots and cuts but prescribes no grammar, so nothing here restudies an export.
- **Traceable sources 1–3:** each observation carries its kind and locator. Uncertain values keep their ranges, and unavailable sources are listed under [unavailable](#unavailable).
